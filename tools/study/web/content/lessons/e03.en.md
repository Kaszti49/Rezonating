## @bench
**2026-11-03, pc-10.** After the press test the boss asked: "Why a series resistor? Why not a divider?" His version: 220 Ω from 3.3 V to a midpoint, 470 Ω from the midpoint to GND, the load cell hung on the midpoint. Calculated, it gives ≈ 1.97 V on the cell and draws about 6 mA. Ours (470 Ω + 220 Ω in series, then the cell) gives about 2.0 V and draws 1.84 mA. By the end you'll be able to work out both by hand and say what each costs.

## @intuition
### Series: one road, one traffic

Put two narrow sections of pipe one after the other. All the water goes through both, and the push is shared. Two [[series]] resistors act like one bigger resistor: they simply add. Our 470 Ω + 220 Ω = 690 Ω.

### Parallel: two roads side by side

Put two pipes side by side between the same two points. They share the same push, and the water splits between them. More roads, easier flow: [[parallel]] resistors are *smaller* than either one. Two equal resistors give half.

- Two 1 kΩ in parallel: 500 Ω.
- 470 Ω with 1100 Ω in parallel: about 329 Ω (smaller than the smaller one).

### The divider: sharing the push

Two resistors in series share the voltage in proportion to their sizes. The bigger one takes the bigger share. That is a [[voltage-divider|voltage divider]]. Tap the midpoint and you get a lower voltage than the supply.

This is our rig: the "top" is the 690 Ω and the "bottom" is the cell. Switch the wiring to the boss's and compare the numbers.

### The catch: a load changes the divider

A divider's midpoint is only the textbook number if nothing is hanging on it. Hang a load there and it joins the lower resistor in parallel. The lower part gets smaller, and the midpoint **sags**. That is [[loading]].

{{predict:0}}

> [!warn] Where the picture breaks. "Voltage is fixed by the supply" is only true at the very top of the chain. Everywhere else, it depends on what's connected.

### Which one is better?

Both give about 2 V. The difference is how "stiff" the feed is. A feed with a small [[source-resistance|source resistance]] holds its voltage when the load changes; a large one doesn't.

{{predict:1}}

The price of the boss's version is the current: about 6 mA instead of 1.84 mA. The ESP32's 3.3 V rail can supply it, but it is three times more heat in the resistors and three times more load on the DevKit's regulator (E17).

## @engineer
### Formulas

$$ R_series = R1 + R2 + …        1/R_par = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + … $$

$$ R_par (two) = R1 · R2 / (R1 + R2) $$

$$ V_out = V_in · R2 / (R1 + R2)        (unloaded) $$

With a load RL on the output: replace R2 by R2 ∥ RL, then divide.

## @datasheet
### What the parts say

Resistors come with a **tolerance**, so the numbers above are nominal. With 5 % on both series parts (470 and 220), the 690 Ω can be 655–725 Ω, and the bridge voltage 1.99–2.07 V (calculated, bridge 1100 Ω). That spread is harmless here, because the sense lines stay near 1.0 V.

| Parameter | Where | Why we care |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Resistor tolerance | the resistor's datasheet (colour band) | the divider ratio is only as good as the parts |
| Cell input resistance | the YZC-133 listing: about 1066 Ω input, about 1000 Ω output (shop listing, not a manufacturer datasheet); we read 1070–1120 Ω | the lower leg of both feeds |
| Cell excitation | the listing says 3–10 V; we run it at about 2.0 V, **below that range** | the output is proportionally smaller; the cell works, but its accuracy at 2 V isn't specified |
| ADS131M02 input range | "Absolute Maximum Ratings" and the input-range specification: at gain ≥ 8 inputs between AGND −1.3 V and AVDD −1.8 V (≈ 1.5 V at 3.3 V) | why a 3.3 V feed is not allowed |

So the reason for either feed is a datasheet limit, not taste: the bridge's sense lines sit at half of its supply, and the ADC's input range at gain 128 stops at about 1.5 V. We have not measured the real sense-line voltages yet; the daily note and the build note give calculated values.
