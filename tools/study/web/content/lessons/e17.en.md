## @bench
**The node is on a bench supply of USB's 5 V.** On the DevKit, an AMS1117 turns that 5 V into the 3.3 V that the ESP32, the ADC 15 Click and the load-cell excitation share: one rail for everything. The question comes as soon as we add Wi-Fi and the SD card: is one small regulator enough, and will it get hot? This lesson lets you answer with a table and two formulas, and tells you what changes when we move to our own PCB.

## @intuition
### A regulator is a tap that keeps the pressure steady

Picture the supply as a water tank with a higher pressure than you need. An [[ldo|LDO regulator]] is a valve that closes just enough to hold the outlet at a steady lower pressure. Every drop of water still flows through it. The pressure you throw away does not vanish: the valve gets warm. So the same current flows in and out, and the voltage difference becomes heat.

{{predict:0}}

A [[switching-regulator|switching regulator]] works differently. It chops the input into short pulses and lets an inductor smooth them. Little energy is wasted, but the chopping leaves a ripple at the switching frequency. The analogy breaks here: a valve has no ripple.

### Why not just use the switching one everywhere?

Our ADC measures microvolts. Ripple that gets onto the analog supply or into the inputs shows up as noise in the data. A linear regulator wastes more energy but is quiet. For a node that only needs a few hundred milliamps, the quiet one is usually the right start. The compromise is a switching stage first and a small LDO after it for the analog part. Whether we do that is not decided.

{{predict:1}}

### Decoupling: a tiny battery at every pin

A chip does not draw current evenly. When many transistors switch together it takes a sharp spike, in nanoseconds. A regulator several centimetres away cannot answer that fast. The [[decoupling|decoupling capacitor]] next to the pin holds a little charge for that moment, then refills slowly. A larger [[bulk-capacitor|bulk capacitor]] near the regulator or the connector covers the slower swings.

The AMS1117 is rated 800 mA in this site's widget (check the datasheet). We are far below it. The heat is the issue, not the current.

### Dropout

[[dropout|Dropout]] is the smallest Vin − Vout at which the regulator still holds its output. With about 1.1 V (check the datasheet), 3.3 V out needs at least about 4.4 V in. USB gives 5 V nominal, minus whatever the cable and port lose. That is not much margin, and the widget shows it in red when the margin goes negative.

### Our own PCB: what we know and what we don't

| Question | Status |
| --- | --- |
| Rails on the ADS131M04 | AVDD and DVDD, both 3.3 V (verified pin table) |
| CAP pin | 220 nF to DGND, for the ADC's internal digital LDO |
| Head ports | +3.3 V on RJ45 pins 4–5, GND on 7–8 |
| Input to the node (USB, DC jack) | not decided |
| Separate analog regulator or ferrite bead for AVDD | not decided |
| Wi-Fi on the node | not decided: it is the biggest load |

Head cable loss is simple Ohm's law. A 24 AWG copper conductor is about 84 Ω/km (a textbook figure). 20 m of one conductor is about 1.7 Ω. With two wires in parallel for +3.3 V and two for GND, the loop is about 1.7 Ω. A head drawing 10 mA (an example, not a measurement) loses about 17 mV. The ADXL355 is a digital head, so its supply droop matters for its own regulator, not for our analog signal. Check its current in its datasheet.

## @datasheet
### What to read in a regulator datasheet

| Section | What to take |
| --- | --- |
| "Absolute Maximum Ratings" | the highest Vin it survives: not the working range |
| "Electrical Characteristics" | dropout at your current, line and load regulation, quiescent current |
| "Thermal Information" | thermal resistance of the package; then ΔT = P × R_θ |
| "Application Information" | the output capacitor it needs to be stable |

An LDO needs a specific output capacitor to be stable; many parts become unstable with the wrong kind. For the AMS1117 read the required capacitor and its ESR range in the datasheet before you choose parts. Do not copy a value from memory.

### In the ADS131M04 datasheet

- **Pin table (Table 5-1):** AVDD/AGND and DVDD/DGND are separate pins; CAP needs 220 nF to DGND.
- **"Absolute Maximum Ratings":** inputs AGND − 1.6 V to AVDD + 0.3 V. This is why the supply value matters for the input window: if AVDD sags, the upper limit sags with it.
- **"Recommended Operating Conditions"** gives the allowed supply range: read the min and max, not the typical.
- The datasheet's layout section says where to put the supply bypass caps. Take that from the document, and see E20.
