## @bench
**The ADS131M04 for our PCB is a 20-pin TSSOP with 0.65 mm pitch.** Before one trace is drawn, someone must know which pin takes the clock, which takes the 220 nF capacitor, and which supply goes where. That answer is in the datasheet PDF. And on the bench, the ADC 15 Click gives us 16 mikroBUS pins that map to the ESP32 pins in a wiring table. Today you learn to find the answer in a document you didn't write: a datasheet, a pin table, a schematic.

## @intuition
### A datasheet is a contract, not a story

Think of a datasheet as a contract between the chip maker and you. The maker promises certain behaviour *if* you stay inside certain conditions. Some pages are promises (the numbers in tables), some are suggestions (the typical application circuit), and some are warnings (absolute maximum ratings). Reading well means knowing which kind of page you have open.

{{predict:0}}

### Typical is not a promise

Tables have up to three columns: min, typ and max. Typical is what a normal part does. Min and max are the maker's guarantee. If your design only works at "typical", it works on some boards and fails on others.

{{predict:1}}

### A schematic is a map of connections, not a photo

A [[schematic]] shows which pins are joined. It does not show where parts sit. Each part gets a [[reference-designator|reference designator]]: R for resistor, C for capacitor, U for ICs, J for connectors. Wires that would cross a page are given a [[net-label|net label]]: two wires with the same name are one wire. The analogy to a metro map works: it shows connections and stops, not distances.

## @engineer
### A datasheet in the order you should read it

1. **First page / Features:** what the part is and its headline numbers. Check you have the right part and package.
2. **Pin configuration and functions:** names, numbers and meanings of all pins.
3. **Absolute Maximum Ratings:** the damage limits.
4. **Recommended Operating Conditions:** the range where the specs hold.
5. **Electrical Characteristics:** the numbers, with min/typ/max and test conditions.
6. **Timing requirements:** setup and hold times (E16).
7. **Typical Application:** a working circuit to copy parts of.
8. **Layout guidelines:** where to put capacitors and how to route (E20).

This is from the verified pinout in our part data (TI datasheet Table 5-1). The pin numbers belong in the datasheet table; take them from there.

### The ADC 15 Click, pin by pin

The Click has two 8-pin mikroBUS headers, and a pair of screw terminals for the analog inputs.

| Click pin | Goes to | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CS1 | ADC CS | SPI mode 1 |
| CS2 (mikroBUS PWM) | LTC6903 CS | SPI mode 0 |
| SCK | both chips | shared clock |
| SDO | ADC DOUT → MCU MISO | |
| SDI | ADC DIN and LTC6903 ← MCU MOSI | |

| 3V3, GND | the only supply | 3.3 V logic only |

> [!ok] Tip from our ADXL355 note: Analog Devices writes I²C with a superscript 2 (I²C). A PDF search for "I2C" misses it. Search "SCL", "ASEL" or "VSSIO".

### Three habits

- **Note the document and section** next to every number you copy into the vault or into code, as the firmware does ("SBAS853A 8.5.1"). If you don't know the page, name the section.
- **Check the package.** The same chip often comes in several packages with different pinouts. Our ADS131M04IPWR is the TSSOP-20.
- **Never trust a number from memory.** If a value is not in front of you, write "check the datasheet" and look it up.
