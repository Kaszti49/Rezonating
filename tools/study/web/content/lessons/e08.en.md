## @bench
**Two SM-24 geophones are ordered** (€78.95 each) and not here yet. The part note says 28.8 V/(m/s), and the ADC at gain 32 only takes ±37.5 mV. Before the parts arrive we want to know: how big a floor movement fills the ADC, why does the parts list have a 1339 Ω shunt resistor, and what does the curve with the 10 Hz hump mean?

By the end you can answer all three from a few numbers and the live diagram.

## @intuition
### A magnet that refuses to move

Hang a heavy magnet on springs inside a coil. Shake the case: the case moves with the floor, but the heavy magnet lags behind, so the magnet and coil move **relative** to each other. That is the whole sensor.

A magnet moving past a coil makes a voltage in it. That is [[faraday|Faraday's law]]: faster movement, bigger voltage. So the output is proportional to **velocity**, not position. A [[geophone]] is a velocity sensor.

### Why only above 10 Hz

A mass on a spring has a natural rhythm, here 10 Hz (its [[natural-frequency]]). Shake much slower than that and the mass simply rides along with the case, so there is almost no relative motion and almost no voltage. Shake much faster and the mass stands still while the case moves: full signal. So the useful range starts around 10 Hz. The listing says 10–240 Hz.

{{predict:0}}

### Ringing and damping

Push a child on a swing at its own rhythm and the swing goes high. The geophone does the same near 10 Hz: it **rings**. [[damping]] is the brake that stops the ringing. The geophone's brake is electrical: connect a [[shunt]] resistor across the coil, a current flows, and the coil pushes back on the magnet.

{{w:geophone zeta=0.6 f=10 vel=0.1}}

Switch between the three damping values and sweep the frequency. Open circuit: a tall peak. With the shunt: a calmer hump.

{{predict:1}}

> [!warn] The swing analogy breaks in one place: the damping here costs signal. A shunt also divides the output voltage with the coil's own resistance, so more braking can mean a smaller output.

## @engineer
### Faraday and inductance

For one turn, the induced voltage is V = −dΦ/dt. For a coil in a magnetic field B moving at speed v, the idea is **V ∝ B · length of wire · v**. All of that is packed into one number, the sensitivity S₀ = 28.8 V/(m/s).

The coil also has [[inductance]] (V = L · dI/dt) and resistance (≈ 375 Ω). We don't have its inductance in our notes: check the datasheet before designing a filter around it.

### What the ADC sees

$$ V_out = S · v        v in m/s,  S in V/(m/s) $$

{{calc:0}}

> [!ok] 1.3 mm/s is a calculated limit, not a measurement. Once the SM-24s arrive, the first recording tells us how large real footsteps are, and we can choose the gain.

### The second-order model

The site diagram uses a mass-spring model. With r = f / f₀ (f₀ = 10 Hz) the relative sensitivity is:

$$ H(f) = r² / √((1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²) $$

| Frequency | ζ = 0.25 | ζ = 0.60 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 Hz | 0.04 | 0.04 |
| 10 Hz | 2.0 | 0.83 |
| 40 Hz | 1.06 | 1.02 |
| 100 Hz | 1.01 | 1.00 |

(All calculated from the formula above.) At 10 Hz, H = 1 / (2ζ).

{{calc:2}}

### The shunt as a divider

The shunt Rs and the coil Rc share the generated voltage. If the 28.8 V/(m/s) is the open-circuit figure, the terminal sensitivity with a shunt is:

$$ S_term = S₀ · Rs / (Rs + Rc) $$

{{calc:1}}

It is a voltage divider, as in E03. The price of damping is about 22 % of the signal. The damping numbers (below) say what it buys.

### Damping values

| Shunt | ζ |
| --- | --- |
| open circuit | 0.25 |
| 1339 Ω | 0.60 |
| 1000 Ω | 0.69 |
