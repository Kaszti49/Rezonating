## @bench
**Our own PCB, still on paper.** The ADS131M04 pin table says pin 18 (CAP) wants **220 nF to DGND**. Every guide also says "put 100 nF at each supply pin". The bench assortment holds 100 nF ceramics and a 10 µF electrolytic with a stripe on it. Capacitors do nothing at DC, so why are there so many, which one goes where, and which way round?

By the end you can answer all three, and you can work out how fast any RC circuit settles.

## @intuition
### A capacitor is a small water tank

Think of [[current]] as water and [[voltage]] as the water level. A [[capacitor]] is a tank in the pipe. Water (charge) flows in and the level rises; water flows out and it drops. A bigger tank (more capacitance, in [[farad|farads]]) needs more water for the same rise in level.

- A steady level: no water moves. A charged capacitor passes **no steady current**. It blocks DC.
- A level that keeps changing: water keeps sloshing in and out. It passes **AC**.

The analogy breaks in one place: a real tank can overflow quietly, but a capacitor has a **voltage rating**. Above it, the part is damaged.

### Charging takes time

Fill the tank through a narrow pipe (a [[resistor]]) and it fills fast at first, then slower as the levels come closer. The time scale is the [[time-constant|time constant]] τ = R · C. After one τ the tank is 63 % full.

### Why a tank next to every chip

A chip does not draw current smoothly. Each time its logic switches, it pulls a short, sharp spike. The supply trace back to the regulator is long and a little inductive, so it can't answer in time. A small tank right at the supply pin can. That is [[decoupling]]: 100 nF of [[ceramic-cap|ceramic]] at each supply pin.

{{predict:1}}

### The polarised kind

The [[electrolytic|electrolytic capacitor]] stores much more (our bench has 10 µF), but it has a plus and a minus side. The **stripe marks the minus leg**. Wrong way round, it heats and can burst. Use it as a big reservoir for slow changes and keep the ceramic for the fast ones.

> [!warn] A capacitor is not a battery you can ignore. A big one charged to a few volts can still hold energy: discharge before you touch the board with a probe in resistance mode.

## @engineer
### Charge, current, energy

$$ Q = C · V        I = C · dV/dt        E = ½ · C · V² $$

At 3.3 V, a 100 nF capacitor holds Q = 330 nC and E ≈ 0.54 µJ. Tiny, but a chip's spike is tiny too.

### RC step response

Through a resistor from a voltage step Vs:

$$ V(t) = Vs · (1 − e^(−t / τ)),        τ = R · C $$

### Capacitors we have and need

| Part | Where | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 100 nF ceramic ('104') | next to each chip's supply pin | have it (assortment) |
| 10 µF electrolytic | bulk reservoir on a supply | have it; stripe = − |
| 220 nF | ADS131M04 pin 18 (CAP) to DGND | not in the assortment note: add it to the PCB parts list |

Capacitors in parallel add (100 nF + 10 µF ≈ 10.1 µF). That is why a board often carries a big and a small one together: the big one covers slow changes, the small one covers fast ones.

### Reading the fault: a slow supply

If a supply rail wobbles when a chip starts working, suspect missing or distant decoupling. Look at the rail with the scope (or the ADC) at the moment of the activity. A tiny dip of tens of mV at the chip pin is normal. A visible sag is not.

### The capacitors themselves

| Parameter | Ceramic (MLCC) | Electrolytic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Polarity | none | **yes**, stripe = − |
| Voltage rating | printed or in the datasheet; stay well under it | on the can; stay well under it |
| Dielectric | X7R, C0G, Y5V …: affects temperature and DC-bias change | n/a |
| Tolerance | ±10 % or ±20 % typical | ±20 % typical |

Ceramics can lose a good part of their capacitance with DC voltage on them (DC bias), depending on the dielectric and size. We have no datasheet for the assortment parts: check the marking, and use a known part with a datasheet on the PCB. Our bench values are "have it", not "measured".
