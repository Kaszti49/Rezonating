## @bench
**Decision D-016** (the 2 kSPS record rate) is written in decibels: at 1 kSPS the ADC's own filter attenuates "only −4.4 dB" at 500 Hz, while at 2 kSPS the 0–400 Hz band loses "only −0.9 dB" and 1600–2400 Hz is cut "by at least 17.5 dB". To follow why Kaszti chose 2 kSPS, you have to read those numbers.

By the end of this lesson you can turn each of them back into "how much signal is left", and describe any signal by the frequencies in it.

## @intuition
### A sine wave is a pure note

A tuning fork makes the simplest vibration there is: a [[sine-wave|sine wave]]. Three numbers describe it:

- **how big**: the amplitude A
- **how fast**: the [[frequency]] f in hertz (cycles per second). The time for one cycle is the [[period]] T = 1 / f
- **where it starts**: the [[phase]] φ

A floor footstep isn't a pure note, but it is made of them. Our working range for footsteps is roughly 10–300 Hz, and the SM-24 is listed for 10–240 Hz.

### Any signal is a recipe of sine waves

Strike a drum and you hear one sound, but it is a mixture of notes. The [[spectrum]] is the recipe: for each frequency, how much is in the mix. A single sine is one spike. A footstep is a broad hump at the low end. Noise is a low floor across everything.

> [!warn] A spectrum hides time. It tells you what frequencies appeared, not when. For a footstep, we look at both: the waveform and its spectrum.

### Decibels: a ruler that multiplies

Signals in our chain range from millivolts to volts, and a filter may cut a tone to a millionth. On a normal ruler the small numbers are lost. The [[decibel]] ruler is logarithmic: every **20 dB** is a factor of 10 in voltage.

| dB | Amplitude factor |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | 1 |
| −6 | ≈ 0.5 |
| −20 | 0.1 |
| −40 | 0.01 |

{{predict:0}}

Two stages one after the other multiply their factors, which in dB means **adding** the numbers. That's the main reason engineers use them.

### Peak and RMS

The [[rms|RMS]] value is the "effective" size. For a sine:

$$ RMS = A / √2 ≈ 0.707 · A $$

{{calc:0}}

Noise figures in datasheets are quoted in RMS, so you will need this conversion. Peak-to-peak is 2 · A.

### Decibels

$$ dB = 20 · log10(V2 / V1)        ratio = 10^(dB / 20) $$

For power the factor is 10 instead of 20. Use 20 for our volts and sample values.

### Reading D-016 in plain numbers

| Statement in D-016 | dB | Amplitude left |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 kSPS, 500 Hz, ADC filter | −4.4 | 60 % |
| 2 kSPS, edge of 0–400 Hz band | −0.9 | 90 % |
| 2 kSPS, 1600–2400 Hz, at least | −17.5 | 13 % or less |

Note the direction: at 1 kSPS the unwanted 500–800 Hz energy keeps about 60 % of its amplitude and folds into the band. At 2 kSPS the part that could fold into 0–400 Hz is cut to 13 % or less by the ADC and then crushed by the FIR (E10).

## @datasheet
### What to look for in a datasheet

- **Noise** is given as µVrms (RMS in a bandwidth) or nV/√Hz (per square root of hertz). The second is a spectral density: multiply by √bandwidth to get RMS.
- **Digital filter response** of the ADS131M0x is plotted in dB against frequency. D-016 used it; its numbers are a "datasheet-based calculation", not a measurement of our board.
- A **Bode plot** (E10) is gain in dB on the vertical axis and frequency on a log horizontal axis.

### dBFS

ADC people often write dBFS: dB relative to full scale. For us full scale is ±1.2 V / gain (±37.5 mV at gain 32). A tone at −20 dBFS is one tenth of full scale.
