## @intuition
### The problem: a tiny change on a big number

A [[strain-gauge|strain gauge]] is a thin foil zig-zag glued to a metal beam. Bend the beam, and the foil stretches a little, and its resistance rises by a few hundred parts per million ([[ppm]]). A 1000 Ω gauge changes by a fraction of an ohm. Measuring that change as an absolute resistance means catching 0.1 % of a big number, and any temperature drift is bigger than the signal.

Picture two people weighing a feather by weighing themselves with and without it. You'd need a precise scale and they'd never stand still. Better to put both on a see-saw and see which side tips.

{{predict:0}}

### The bridge: a see-saw for voltages

A [[wheatstone-bridge|Wheatstone bridge]] is two [[voltage-divider|voltage dividers]] side by side from the same supply. When they match, their midpoints are at the same voltage and the difference between them is zero. Change one gauge and the see-saw tips: the difference becomes a few millivolts, on a zero baseline, not on top of 1 V.

Our cell has four gauges: two get stretched when you push, two get compressed. Everything that goes the same way (temperature, supply wiggles) cancels, and everything that goes the opposite way (the bending) adds. That is a *full* bridge.

{{w:bridge vex=2 kg=1 gain=128}}

Slide the load and watch the bar: at gain 128 the ADC's range is only ±9.375 mV, and 5 kg at 2 V takes about a fifth of it.

> [!warn] Where the analogy breaks. A see-saw is balanced by weights; a bridge is balanced by resistance ratios, and real cells are never perfectly balanced. The leftover imbalance at zero load is the **offset**.

### Reading the sign

The ADC measures green minus white. Press harder and the number rises; swap the wires and it falls.

## @engineer
### Bridge formulas

Four resistances R1–R4 in a diamond, excitation Vex across the top and bottom corners; the output is the difference between the two midpoints:

$$ Vout = Vex · ( R3 / (R3 + R4) − R2 / (R1 + R2) ) $$

If all four are equal, Vout = 0. For a full bridge with four active gauges, two +ΔR and two −ΔR:

$$ Vout = Vex · ΔR / R $$

A single active gauge (quarter bridge) gives roughly Vex · ΔR / (4R). The gauge itself follows ΔR/R = GF · ε, where ε is the strain and GF is the gauge factor, textbook value about 2 for metal foil (the YZC-133's value isn't in our listing, so check the datasheet).

### Why gain 128

| Gain | Full scale | 2 mV is… |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | ±1.2 V | 0.17 % of the range |
| 4 | ±300 mV | about 0.7 % |
| 128 | ±9.375 mV | about 21 % |

At low gain, the signal would use a handful of the ADC's useful bits, and its noise would drown it. The programmable-gain amplifier ([[pga]]) boosts the signal first.

### Offset and drift

The cell's listing gives a zero balance of ±0.1 mV/V: at 2 V that is up to ±0.2 mV, the equivalent of ±0.5 kg. On the bench the resting value moved: about −20 µV during the press test, about 86 µV ± 21 earlier the same day (ch0, daily note). We don't yet know how much of that is the cell, the supply, the ADC's own [[offset]] or handling. The practical answer is to **tare**: measure the resting value and subtract it. Drift with temperature isn't measured; its number isn't in our listing either.

The signal scales with the excitation (the bridge is [[ratiometric]]). The Click measures against a fixed 1.2 V reference, not against the bridge supply, so noise and drift on the 3.3 V rail show up in the reading (build note). That doesn't matter for vibration; it would for absolute weight.

### The open spikes

A spike of −4.5 mV equals about −11 kg at 0.4 mV/kg, far beyond what a 5 kg cell should show, and negative full scale is −9375 µV. A knock should show as a two-sided wiggle. Candidate causes in the vault: a loose contact on the green or white wire, or something shared with CH2 (earlier CH1 and CH2 moved together when the rig was handled). The wiggle test hasn't been done.

### What this means for us

The bridge's sense lines sit at half of the excitation. A 3.3 V excitation would give 1.65 V there, above the ≈ 1.5 V limit, so we cut the excitation to about 2 V with a series resistor, and accept 40 % less signal (2.0 mV instead of 3.3 mV at 5 kg). Gain 128 gives back what that cost. If the project needs real weights, the cell must be calibrated with known masses; today, the cell is a known test signal for the chain.
