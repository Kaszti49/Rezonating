## @bench
**2026-11-03, pc-10.** The Handoff said: "3.3 V on one end of the resistor, 0 V on the other". Where did the 3.3 V go? If you know that the voltages around a loop must add up, the answer is on the page before you touch a probe: it dropped across the one part that was too big. This lesson gives you two rules that solve every small circuit on our bench and catch wrong readings.

## @intuition
### Rule 1: what comes in must go out (KCL)

Picture a pipe junction. The water coming into the junction per second equals the water leaving it, because water can't pile up in a joint or vanish. Electric charge behaves the same way. A junction is a [[node]]. This is Kirchhoff's current law ([[kcl|KCL]]).

On the boss's divider the midpoint is such a node: one road comes in (through the 220 Ω), two leave (the 470 Ω and the load cell).

{{predict:0}}

### Rule 2: around any loop, the ups and downs cancel (KVL)

Walk around a hiking loop and come back to the start: the climbing and the descending add up to zero. A [[loop]] in a circuit is the same walk: the supply lifts you by 3.3 V, and every part drops you a bit, until you arrive back at 0 V (GND). This is the voltage law ([[kvl|KVL]]).

For the series rig: the supply gives +3.3 V; the 690 Ω takes about 1.27 V; the bridge takes about 2.03 V. 3.3 − 1.27 − 2.03 = 0.

In the diagram read the currents in the three branches and check that two of them add up to the third.

> [!warn] Where the picture breaks. "Walking up and down" hides that you have to choose the same direction for each part, and that voltages have signs. If you write an equation and a drop comes out negative, you only chose the opposite arrow, not made a mistake.

### What a node really is on a breadboard

A [[node]] is every point connected by wire (zero resistance). On a [[breadboard]] the five holes of one short strip are one node; so is a long power rail. Two parts in the same strip are connected, whether or not you meant it.

## @engineer
### The two laws

$$ KCL:    Σ I_in = Σ I_out        KVL:    Σ V (around a loop) = 0 $$

Procedure for a small circuit:

1. Name the nodes (supply, midpoint, GND).
2. Pick a current direction for each branch (any; a negative answer just means the other way).
3. KCL at every node except GND.
4. KVL around every independent loop.
5. Ohm's law for each part: V = I · R.

### Series rig (one loop, no node)

One loop, so one current. KVL: 3.3 V = I · 690 + I · 1100, giving I = 1.844 mA. Check: 1.844 mA × 690 Ω = 1.272 V and × 1100 Ω = 2.028 V, which sum to 3.300 V.

### Boss's divider (one node, two loops)

Unknowns: I1 through the 220 Ω, I2 through the 470 Ω, I3 through the cell (take 1100 Ω).

- KCL at the midpoint: I1 = I2 + I3.
- KVL, left loop: 3.3 = 220 · I1 + 470 · I2.
- KVL, right loop: 470 · I2 = 1100 · I3 (the same midpoint voltage).

Solving gives I1 ≈ 6.0 mA, I2 ≈ 4.2 mA, I3 ≈ 1.8 mA, midpoint ≈ 1.98 V (calculated, nothing was measured).

### Using the laws on readings

A meter reading must obey KVL. If you measure the drop across each part in a loop and the sum isn't the supply, one of the readings is wrong (a wrong range, a wrong node, or another part hiding in the loop). In the 680 kΩ fault the sum worked out: the resistor dropped almost all the 3.3 V.

## @datasheet
### Where KCL and KVL show up in a datasheet

The laws aren't printed in datasheets, but they decide how you read them:

- **Supply current** (the "Electrical Characteristics" sections): the node at 3.3 V must supply the sum of all the chips' currents (KCL). Adding them is the power budget in E17. Our datasheets list the numbers; we don't copy them here, so check the ADS131M02 and ESP32-S3 datasheets.
- **Absolute Maximum Ratings** include input current limits per pin. Any current into a clamp pin must come out of somewhere (KCL), which is why a series resistor before a protected pin works (E11).
- **Typical application circuits** show every node by name. A net label, such as `3V3`, is the same node everywhere on the schematic.

### Reading the ADC Click's inputs

Our bridge's sense wires go to the ADS131M02 inputs, which draw almost no current compared with the bridge (high input impedance; check the datasheet's input current figure). That's why we could treat the bridge as a plain two-resistor chain above, and why the sense lines show half the bridge voltage without loading it.

### Checklist for a new circuit

1. Mark all nodes with names.
2. Write KCL for each node.
3. Check KVL around each loop with your meter before you power up the next stage.
