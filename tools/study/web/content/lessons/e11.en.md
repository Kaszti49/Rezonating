## @bench
**A rule from the Handoff:** the ADS131M0x inputs survive only from AGND − 1.6 V to AVDD + 0.3 V, and piezo heads must be clamped and buffered. On a 3.3 V supply that is −1.6 V to 3.6 V. A piezo disc that someone hits with a knuckle can go far beyond it. How do we keep a 24-bit ADC alive?

By the end of this lesson you can draw the protection, size its resistor, and check it against the limit.

## @intuition
### A diode is a one-way valve

A [[diode]] lets current through in one direction and blocks the other. The valve needs a small push to open: the [[forward-voltage|forward voltage]], about 0.3 V for a [[schottky|Schottky]] and about 0.7 V for silicon. The two legs are the [[anode-cathode|anode and cathode]]; the stripe marks the cathode. Current flows anode to cathode.

The valve analogy breaks in two places: a real diode doesn't open sharply (it opens gradually), and the push it needs grows a little as more current flows.

### A glowing valve: the LED

An [[led|LED]] is a diode that gives light while conducting. Its forward voltage is higher, about 2 V for red. It has no built-in limit on current: past its forward voltage it conducts almost freely, so a resistor in series sets how much flows. No resistor: the LED, or the pin feeding it, dies.

{{predict:0}}

### Two valves as a safety fence

Now use the valves as a fence around the ADC pin. One diode leads from the pin to the supply (AVDD), one from the pin to ground (AGND). In normal use neither opens. If the signal goes above the supply by a diode drop, the upper valve opens and **sends the surplus to the supply**; below ground, the lower one does the same. That is a [[clamp]].

The valves hold the voltage, but something has to carry the surplus current, or it will burn them. That is a **series resistor** between the source and the pin: it takes the voltage difference and sets the current.

Slide the piezo peak up and see what happens at the pin. Choose "none" for the diodes and watch it fail.

> [!warn] The diodes protect the pin. They don't make the reading good: above full scale the ADC still [[clipping|clips]].

### The clamp, with numbers

The pin stays between AGND − Vf and AVDD + Vf. With AVDD = 3.3 V:

| Diode | Lower limit | Upper limit | Inside −1.6 … 3.6 V? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| silicon, 0.7 V | −0.7 V | 4.0 V | no: upper side fails |
| Schottky, 0.3 V | −0.3 V | 3.6 V | yes, but exactly at the limit |

The extra current makes Vf larger, so a Schottky at a high current will sit above 0.3 V. Keep the current small and you stay near the paper value. The series resistor does it:

$$ I_peak = (V_peak − V_clamp) / R $$

A bigger R means a safer clamp but also noise and a slower input: R and the pin's capacitance form an RC low-pass (E10). Where exactly to land is a design choice we haven't made.

### Operating range vs. absolute maximum

| Range | Value | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Absolute maximum | AGND − 1.6 V … AVDD + 0.3 V | survive; **no** accuracy promise |
| At gain ≥ 8, keep inputs | AGND − 1.3 V … AVDD − 1.8 V | i.e. −1.3 … 1.5 V at 3.3 V |

The clamp only enforces the first row. To stay in the second, the head must also be biased and buffered (E13).

### Why a buffer

A piezo is a high-impedance source. The series resistor and the ADC input would load it and distort the signal; a buffer ([[buffer]]) copies its voltage and can drive the resistor and the clamp. The buffer's output and its own supply limits protect it too. The design is E13's job.

Never exceed the absolute maximum, even for a moment: the datasheet promises nothing about a part that has. The ADC has its own internal protection diodes, but those are not a design: they are the last line, and the numbers above are what they are rated for.

### The diode you choose

When picking the clamp diode, read its datasheet for:

- **Forward voltage at your current** (a curve, not one number)
- **Reverse leakage**: Schottky diodes leak more, which loads a high-impedance source like a piezo
- **Capacitance**: it adds to the pin and filters the signal
- **Package and part number**: not chosen yet
