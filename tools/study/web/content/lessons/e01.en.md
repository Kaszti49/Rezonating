## @bench
**2026-11-03, pc-10.** The meter read **3.3 V** on one end of a resistor and **0 V** on the other. A fair question from someone starting at zero: "0 V compared to what?" The answer is "to the black probe", and that is the whole lesson in one sentence. By the end you'll know what a voltage reading is, why a current needs a loop, and how to count the charge and energy our bench moves each second.

## @intuition
### Charge: the stuff that moves

Everything is made of atoms, and a [[conductor]] such as copper has electrons that are free to move. Those electrons carry [[charge]]. Normally they wander randomly and nothing happens. Push them all the same way and you have a [[current]].

Think of a pipe already full of water. Push on one end and water comes out of the other end at once. You don't wait for "your" water to arrive.

- The **pipe** is the wire and the loop it forms.
- The **water** is the charge. It is already there; nothing gets used up.
- The **push** is [[voltage]].
- The **flow** per second is the current.

{{w:ohm v=3.3 r=690}}

The dots show current. Slide the voltage: more push, more flow. The dots circle around, and they have to: there is nowhere for charge to pile up.

### Voltage is a difference, never a point

Pressure alone means nothing; what moves water is a pressure *difference* between two places. A voltage is the same. "The 3V3 pin is at 3.3 V" is shorthand for "3.3 V above the point we agreed to call 0 V": [[ground]], GND.

{{predict:0}}

> [!warn] Where the analogy breaks. Real pipes have an absolute pressure; a circuit has only differences, and we choose the zero. Also, the *individual* electrons in a wire drift extremely slowly (a textbook estimate gives far below a millimetre per second at our currents). The push travels fast, not the electrons.

### Current needs a loop

Charge can't pile up and can't disappear. If a supply pushes charge out of one terminal, the same amount must return into the other. Break the loop anywhere (an open joint, a pulled jumper) and the flow stops everywhere.

## @engineer
### Three quantities and their units

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit | On our bench |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Charge | Q | coulomb (C) | 18 mC in 10 s through the bridge |
| Current | I | ampere (A) | ≈ 1.84 mA (calculated, E02) |
| Voltage | V | volt (V) | 3.3 V supply, ≈ 2.0 V on the bridge |

$$ I = Q / t        W = Q · V        P = W / t = V · I $$

One coulomb per second is one ampere. One joule per coulomb is one volt. Prefixes: m = 10⁻³, µ = 10⁻⁶, k = 10³. Our load-cell output is a few hundred µV; our current is a few mA. Convert to base units (A, V, C) before you multiply.

### Energy and power

The supply gives energy to the charge it pushes. One joule per coulomb is one volt, so charge Q pushed through 3.3 V picks up Q · 3.3 J. That energy ends up as heat in the resistors.

{{calc:2}}

Divide by the time and you have power, P = V · I. E02 builds on that.

### Reading "volts" on the bench

When a note says *ch0 ≈ −20 µV*, it means the voltage between the ADC input pair AIN0P and AIN0N (a [[differential]] reading), not "20 µV at a point". When the Handoff says *3.3 V on the ESP end of the resistor*, it means between that end and GND, with the black probe on GND.

> [!ok] Habit: always say both points. "From the resistor's ESP end to GND: 3.3 V."

## @datasheet
### Where these quantities live in a datasheet

The same three ideas appear in every datasheet we use:

- **Absolute Maximum Ratings:** voltages are given *relative to a named reference pin*. For the ADS131M0x the analog inputs are limited relative to AGND: from AGND −1.6 V up to AVDD +0.3 V (as recorded in our CLAUDE.md; confirm in the datasheet at PCB review).
- **Analog and digital grounds:** AGND and DGND are separate pins on the ADC. Each voltage is measured against the matching one. Where they join on the board is a PCB question (E14, E20).
- **Currents** appear as supply current (analog and digital supply) and as per-pin limits. The sign gives the direction; check each datasheet's own convention, because it isn't the same everywhere.

### Units, exactly

Since the 2019 redefinition of the SI, the ampere is fixed by defining the elementary charge as exactly 1.602 176 634 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. We used 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; the extra digits don't matter for our work.

### What this means for us

A datasheet voltage with no stated reference is incomplete. When you read "3.3 V", ask: "above which pin?" When we later size the clamp for a piezo head (E11), the ADC's limits are voltages against AGND, and the current the clamp must carry follows from Ohm's law (E02).
