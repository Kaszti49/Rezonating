**2026-11-03.** The boss asked why we read the load cell with the ADC 15 Click and not the HX711. The build note's answer starts with one number: the HX711 gives 10 or 80 samples per second, the Click 2000. At 80 SPS the HX711 sees only below 40 Hz, and footsteps carry signal up to a few hundred Hz. The same day the HX711 rig returned 8388607 on every read.

By the end you can explain both facts: what a sample rate lets you see, and what a stuck number means.

## @intuition
### Sampling is taking photographs of a moving thing

Film a spinning wheel with a camera. If the frame rate is much faster than the spin, you see the wheel turning properly. If it is too slow, the wheel seems to turn slowly, or backwards. That wrong, slower motion is an [[aliasing|alias]].

The rule: to see a frequency you need **more than two samples per cycle**. Half the sample rate is the [[nyquist|Nyquist frequency]]. Anything faster does not disappear; it folds back below Nyquist as a false, slower frequency.

{{w:sampling f=700 fs=1000}}

Move the tone above 500 Hz at 1 kSPS and watch where the alias lands. Try the HX711 preset (80 SPS).

{{predict:0}}

Where the film analogy breaks: a camera blurs fast motion before it is stored; an ADC without a filter does not. The fix is to **filter before sampling** (an [[anti-alias]] filter), or to sample much faster and filter in the digital domain, which is what the ADS131M0x does.

### Quantization: rounding to a ladder

An ADC does not measure smoothly. It rounds the voltage to the nearest rung of a ladder. A 24-bit ladder has 16.7 million rungs between −full scale and +full scale; the gap between rungs is one [[lsb|LSB]].

{{w:quantize bits=24 gain=128 amp=0.0004}}

Lower the bits and see the staircase. At 24 bits and gain 128, the rungs are about a nanovolt apart, so far finer than the noise that you will never see them. What matters is not the bit count but how many bits are above the noise: the [[enob|ENOB]].

### Out of range

The ladder has a top and a bottom rung. Past them the reading sticks. That is [[clipping]]: not data, but a sign the input is outside the range.

## @engineer
### Codes and volts

For a two's-complement 24-bit ADC with full scale ±FS:

$$ LSB = 2·FS / 2²⁴        code = V / LSB $$

The ADS131M02 has FS = ±1.2 V / gain. At gain 128 that is ±9.375 mV and codes ±8388607.

{{calc:0}}

| Quantity | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Full scale at gain 128 | ±9.375 mV |
| LSB at gain 128 | ≈ 1.12 nV |
| Press signal | +400…+900 µV ≈ 358 000 … 805 000 codes |
| Largest code | +8388607 (0x7FFFFF) |
| Smallest code | −8388608 (0x800000) |

The [[dynamic-range|dynamic range]] of an ideal N-bit converter is about 6.02·N + 1.76 dB: 146 dB for 24 bits. Real converters are limited by noise, not by the ladder, so the datasheet's noise figure decides the usable range.

### Aliasing in numbers

$$ f_alias = |f − k · fs|,    k chosen so that 0 ≤ f_alias ≤ fs/2 $$

### Why delta-sigma and oversampling

A [[delta-sigma]] converter samples very fast with a very coarse quantizer, which spreads the quantization noise over a wide band. A digital filter then keeps only the wanted band and throws the rest away, then [[decimation|decimates]]. The ratio of modulator rate to output rate is the OSR (oversampling ratio).

For our chip: output rate = f_CLKIN / 2 / OSR.

{{calc:2}}

A [[sar|SAR]] ADC, the other common type, converts each sample directly by a binary search. It is fast and low-latency, with lower resolution; not what 24-bit vibration needs.

### Why 2 kSPS, then 1 kSPS (D-016)

At 1 kSPS the ADC's own filter attenuates only −4.4 dB at the 500 Hz Nyquist frequency, so vibration at 500–800 Hz folds into the band almost unattenuated. At 2 kSPS the 0–400 Hz band loses only −0.9 dB, and everything that could fold into 0–400 Hz (1600–2400 Hz) is cut by at least 17.5 dB. The firmware's FIR then removes 400–1000 Hz and decimates by 2 to the 1 kSPS the packs expect. (Datasheet-based calculation in D-016.)

## @datasheet
### ADS131M02 / M04 (TI)

- **Electrical Characteristics:** resolution, input-referred noise per gain and OSR, SNR/ENOB where given. We do not quote the noise: read it at gain 128, OSR 2048.
- **Clocking / Digital filter sections:** the data rate is set by CLKIN and the OSR setting; the datasheet's sinc filter response is where D-016's −4.4 dB and −0.9 dB come from. There is no internal oscillator, so the clock must always run.
- **Data format:** 24-bit two's complement, so the extremes are 0x7FFFFF and 0x800000. The SPI frame carries a CRC; the firmware counts bad frames as lost samples.
- **PGA gain** steps 1 to 128 and full scale ±1.2 V / gain.

### HX711 (Avia Semiconductor)

Datasheet: rate 10 or 80 SPS selected by the RATE pin (whether our board has the jumper is not visible on the photo, per the part note); channel A gain 128 or 64, channel B gain 32. We did not check its filter response; do so before relying on it.

### ENOB, a short formula

$$ ENOB = (SINAD − 1.76 dB) / 6.02 dB $$

Example (not our chip): SINAD of 110 dB → ENOB ≈ 18 bits. The top 18 bits are signal, the rest noise. For ours, take SNR from the datasheet and compute.
