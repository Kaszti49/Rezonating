## @bench
**2026-11-03, pc-10.** The [[yzc133]] gave no signal. With the USB plugged in, the meter showed **3.3 V** on the ESP32 end of the "680 Ω" excitation resistor and **0 V** on the load-cell end. The Handoff listed three suspects: an open joint, a short to GND, or the resistor soldered to the wrong wire.

The real cause was none of those. Its colour bands read blue, grey, **yellow**: **680 kΩ**, a thousand times too big. By the end of this lesson you'll be able to work that out from the two voltages alone, before you even look at the bands.

## @intuition
### Resistance is a narrowing in the pipe

Picture [[current]] as water flowing through a pipe, and [[voltage]] as the pressure difference that pushes it. A [[resistor]] is a narrow section. The narrower it is, the less water gets through for the same push.

- Same push, narrower pipe → **less flow**.
- Same pipe, more push → **more flow**, in proportion.

That proportion is [[ohms-law]]: double the voltage and the current doubles; double the resistance and the current halves.

{{w:ohm v=3.3 r=690}}

Drag R up to 680 kΩ with the preset. The dots almost stop. That is the bench fault: a trickle of about 5 µA instead of about 2 mA.

### Two narrowings in a row

The load cell's bridge is *also* a narrowing (about 1100 Ω between red and black). Put the resistor and the bridge in a row and the same water flows through both. The pressure, though, **drops mostly across the narrowest part**.

{{predict:0}}

> [!warn] A voltage is always *between* two points. "0 V on the cell end" really means "the same voltage as the meter's black probe (GND)". It doesn't mean "nothing is there".

### Resistors turn energy into heat

Pushing water through a narrowing takes effort, and in a resistor that effort becomes **heat**. That's [[power]], measured in [[watt|watts]]. A small through-hole resistor is happy up to ¼ W. Our 470 Ω carries about 1.6 mW, so it won't even get warm.

{{predict:1}}

## @engineer
### Ohm's law

$$ V = I · R    ⇔    I = V / R    ⇔    R = V / I $$

Units: volts (V), amperes (A), ohms (Ω). Keep everything in base units while you calculate. Writing mA, kΩ and µA is fine, but convert them before multiplying.

### Our bench, solved

The resistor and the bridge are in **series**, so their resistances add and one current flows through both:

$$ I = V / (R_series + R_bridge) $$

{{calc:0}}

The bridge's share of the voltage is that same current times its own resistance. That makes this a **voltage divider**, which E03 covers properly:

$$ V_bridge = 3.3 V × R_bridge / (R_series + R_bridge) $$

| Series resistor | Current | Bridge voltage | Load cell output at 5 kg (1 mV/V) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 690 Ω (fix) | 1.84 mA | 2.03 V | 2.0 mV |
| 680 kΩ (fault) | 4.8 µA | 5.3 mV | 5.3 µV |

{{calc:2}}

### Power

$$ P = V · I = I² · R = V² / R $$

Pick the form that uses what you know. For a resistor in series with a known current, **I² · R** is the quickest.

{{calc:1}}

> [!ok] Rule of thumb: keep a resistor below **half** its rating. ¼ W parts → under 125 mW.

### Reading the fault from the numbers

With the USB connected and current flowing, the voltage **across** a resistor is I · R. We saw ≈ 3.3 V across the "680 Ω" and ≈ 0 V across the bridge. For a healthy 690 Ω the drop would have been 1.84 mA × 690 Ω ≈ 1.27 V. A 3.3 V drop means that resistor is either open or much larger than the load: two meter readings and Ohm's law point straight at it.

## @datasheet
### What a resistor's "datasheet" says

A generic carbon-film resistor (the kind in our assortment) is specified by:

| Parameter | Typical value | Why we care |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Resistance | E12/E24 series values (470, 680, 220 …) | 690 Ω isn't a standard value, so we made it from 470 + 220 |
| Tolerance | 5 % (gold band), 1 % (brown) | 470 Ω ± 5 % → 446.5 … 493.5 Ω |
| Power rating | 0.25 W at 70 °C, derated above | 1.6 mW is nowhere near |
| Temperature coefficient | ± 200 … 500 ppm/°C (carbon film) | changes the bridge excitation with temperature: one reason to measure ratiometrically |

### The colour code

| Colour | Digit | Multiplier |
| --- | --- | --- |
| black | 0 | × 1 |
| brown | 1 | × 10 |
| red | 2 | × 100 |
| orange | 3 | × 1 k |
| yellow | 4 | × 10 k |
| green | 5 | × 100 k |
| blue | 6 | × 1 M |
| violet | 7 | |
| grey | 8 | |
| white | 9 | |

4-band: digit, digit, **multiplier**, tolerance. **680 Ω = blue grey brown**, **680 kΩ = blue grey yellow**. When in doubt, measure in Ω mode with the power off: it takes 5 seconds and would have saved an hour on 2026-11-03.

### Where this shows up in our datasheets

- **ADS131M02/M04 "Absolute Maximum Ratings"** limits input *current* as well as voltage (a few mA per pin). A series resistor in front of an input (Ohm's law: I = ΔV / R) is how E11 keeps a piezo within it.
- **AMS1117** on the DevKit: dissipation = (Vin − 3.3 V) × I. That's the power formula again, in E17.
