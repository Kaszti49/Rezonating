How to use this guide

This guide teaches the foundations of the first Rezonating prototype in enough depth that you can explain every part to a judge and defend every number. It is written to be read in order, once, and then used as a reference.

What the first prototype is

One ESP32-S3-DEV-N16R8 board. An ADS131M04 analog-to-digital converter (ADC) soldered on a TSSOP-20 to DIP adapter, clocked by a QTM325 8.192 MHz oscillator. Two SM-24 geophones on ADC channels 0 and 1. One ADXL355 accelerometer breakout on I²C. A microSD card that stores WAV recordings and JSON sidecar files. Power comes from the dev board's 3.3 V rail. Everything sits on a breadboard. The goal is to record real floor and machine vibration as early as possible.

Later steps, not built yet: RJ45 head ports with a small EEPROM in every head behind an I²C multiplexer, piezo heads, a KiCad PCB (planned for January and February 2027), and MQTT to Home Assistant. The guide teaches these too, because the first rig is designed so they fit.

Rules of the guide

  • Nothing here is a project result. No recording has been made and no model has been trained when this guide was written. Where a number looks like a result, it is a target, a datasheet value, a calculation, or a labelled assumption.
  • Every number carries a source tag in the places where it matters: datasheet printed in a manufacturer document listed in section 17; repo taken from a file in this repository, usually a draft or a starting guess; calculated worked out in this guide from other numbers (you can redo it); assumed chosen for the example only.
  • Datasheet beats repo. Where a repo file and a datasheet disagree, the guide follows the datasheet and says so in a yellow note.
  • Drafts are drafts. The pin map, the head-port pinout, the schemas and all pack numbers are marked draft v0 in the repo. They can change.

The big picture

The node turns floor or machine vibration into numbers, decides what happened, stores the evidence, and later tells Home Assistant. Every other section is one box in the figure below.

The signal chain, end to end

Follow one footstep through the figure.

  1. Footstep to floor wave. A foot hits the floor. The impact sends vibration through the structure. At a sensor on the floor, the floor surface moves a very small distance, many times per second. The speed of that motion is the velocity, measured in metres per second (m/s).
  2. Geophone: velocity to volts. The SM-24 geophone contains a magnet and a coil on springs. Floor motion makes the coil move relative to the magnet, which induces a voltage proportional to the velocity. The SM-24 sensitivity is 28.8 V per m/s datasheet, so the output is in the microvolt to millivolt range. It needs no power supply.
  3. ADS131M04: volts to numbers. The ADC measures four channels at the same instant and turns each voltage into a 24-bit signed number. The number counts how many tiny voltage steps (LSBs) the input is away from zero.
  4. SPI to the ESP32-S3. The ADC announces a new sample set on a pin called DRDY (data ready). The ESP32-S3 reads all four numbers over SPI, using DMA so the CPU is not busy copying bytes.
  5. ESP32-S3: buffer, trigger, features, model. Samples go into a ring buffer in PSRAM. A trigger (STA/LTA for footsteps) spots an event. Features are computed for a short window. A model turns the features into a label.
  6. Storage and, later, network. Raw samples are written to the microSD card as a WAV file with a JSON sidecar that describes the setup. Events (not raw data) will later go to Home Assistant over MQTT.

The second path is the accelerometer. The ADXL355 is mounted on a machine. It measures acceleration in g, digitises it internally to 20 bits, and the ESP32-S3 reads it over I²C. It never touches the ADC.

03

Vibration physics

A vibration is something moving back and forth. You can describe the same motion by how far it moves, how fast it moves, or how hard it accelerates. Sensors differ in which of these three they respond to, and that decides which sensor fits which job.

Waves in floors

When a foot lands, or a machine runs, it pushes on a structure. The push does not stay at one spot. It travels outward as a wave: each bit of material pushes on its neighbour. A floor is a plate or a set of beams, so several wave types exist; in plates, bending (flexural) waves are common. You do not need the wave equations for Year 1. You need three practical facts.

  • The signal weakens with distance. Energy spreads out and is lost as heat inside the material. Higher frequencies are usually lost faster than low ones, so far from the source the signal is both smaller and smoother.
  • The floor shapes the signal. The same footstep looks different on different floors, because the floor's stiffness, mass and layers decide what rings and what dies out. That is why the project records on its own floor and requires at least two floor types in the dataset.
  • The sensor coupling matters. A spike or a weighted base is how the geophone is coupled to the floor. A poorly coupled sensor misses motion.

Why concrete is a harder case than a wooden floor (qualitative)

A concrete slab is heavy and stiff, and often sits directly on the ground. A heavy slab moves less for the same push, and energy can leak into the ground below. A wooden floor on joists is lighter and more flexible, so it moves more and tends to ring. This is a general tendency, not a measurement of our floors. Whether a concrete slab damps footsteps too much for the geophone to work is the project's top risk; the first recordings will answer it.

Displacement, velocity, acceleration

Take a point on the floor moving back and forth as a pure sine at frequency f (in hertz, cycles per second). Let A be its peak displacement. Then:

x(t) = A · sin(2π f t)   displacement, metres v(t) = 2π f · A · cos(2π f t)   velocity, m/s (derivative of x) a(t) = −(2π f)² · A · sin(2π f t)   acceleration, m/s² (derivative of v)

So the peak values are linked by two simple rules:

vpeak = 2π f · xpeak apeak = 2π f · vpeak = (2π f)² · xpeak

Two things follow. First, for a given displacement, velocity grows in proportion to frequency, and acceleration grows with the square of frequency. Second, the three curves are shifted in time: velocity peaks a quarter period before displacement does (it leads by 90°), and acceleration is the mirror image of displacement (180° apart).

What a footstep looks like as a signal

A footstep is not a steady tone. It is a transient: a sudden onset when the heel strikes, then a decaying oscillation as the floor rings down, then near silence until the next step. At a normal pace there are roughly two steps per second typical. Features of a footstep trace that a classifier can use include how fast the signal rises, how strong it is, how long it lasts, and which frequencies it contains.

  • The signal is short: tens of milliseconds to a few hundred. The footstep-id manifest keeps 100 ms before the trigger and 400 ms after repo.
  • It sits on a noise floor: the sensor, the electronics and the building all add background vibration. Detecting a step means finding a burst that rises clearly above that floor (section 10).
  • Its frequency content depends on the person, the shoe, the pace and the floor. How much of it is useful for telling people apart is exactly what the project has to find out. Nothing is claimed here.

The manifests start with a high-pass filter at 5 Hz and a low-pass at 400 Hz repo. They are starting guesses, not derived from a measured footstep spectrum, because no footstep has been recorded on the rig yet.

Say it to a judge

Vibration can be described by displacement, velocity or acceleration. They are linked by the frequency: velocity is two pi f times displacement, and acceleration is two pi f times velocity. A geophone responds to velocity and an accelerometer to acceleration, so each is strongest in a different frequency range.

Self-check

1. A floor moves with 2 µm peak displacement at 50 Hz. What is the peak velocity?

v = 2π · 50 · 2 µm = 628 µm/s = 0.628 mm/s.

2. Compare acceleration for the same velocity at 10 Hz and 100 Hz.

a = 2π f v, so at 100 Hz the acceleration is 10 times larger than at 10 Hz for the same velocity.

3. When displacement is at its peak, what is the velocity?

Zero. Velocity leads displacement by 90°; the point stops for an instant at the extreme of its motion.

4. What does −20 dB mean for an amplitude?

The amplitude is 10 times smaller (a factor of 0.1).

5. Why do we say a footstep is a transient rather than a tone?

It has a sudden onset and a decaying ring-down, then near silence. A tone would continue at constant amplitude.

04

Sensors

Three sensor types, three physical principles. The geophone responds to velocity, the MEMS accelerometer to acceleration, and the piezo disc to strain. Choosing the right one for each job is the first design decision a judge will probe.

4aSM-24 geophone

How it works

A geophone is a small electrical generator. Inside a case sits a permanent magnet that is fixed to the case. A coil of fine wire hangs on springs in the magnetic field. The coil is the moving mass: 11 g in the SM-24 datasheet.

When the floor moves, the case and magnet move with it. The coil, hanging on springs, stays almost still in space (for vibration faster than the natural frequency; see below). So the coil moves relative to the magnet. A conductor moving in a magnetic field gets a voltage across it (Faraday's law of induction):

e = B · l · vrelB = magnetic flux density, l = length of wire in the field, vrel = relative velocity

B and l are constants of the construction, so the output voltage is proportional to the velocity. The product B·l is the sensitivity: 28.8 V per m/s, ±2.5% datasheet. That is 28.8 µV for a floor velocity of 1 µm/s. A geophone needs no power: it makes its own voltage from the motion.

Natural frequency

The coil on its springs is a mass on a spring, with a natural frequency

fn = (1 / 2π) · √(k / m)

The SM-24's natural frequency is 10 Hz ±2.5% datasheet. With the 11 g moving mass the spring constant works out to k = m·(2π fn)² = 0.011 · 62.83² ≈ 43 N/m calculated.

  • Well above fn the coil stays still and the relative velocity equals the floor velocity. Output is proportional to velocity. This is the useful, flat region.
  • Near fn the mass resonates. The output is boosted or shaped depending on the damping.
  • Well below fn the coil simply follows the case, so the relative velocity falls toward zero and so does the output. The response rolls off at 12 dB per octave (an ideal second-order system).

Damping and why a shunt resistor changes it

Damping describes how quickly the resonance dies away. It is written as a dimensionless number, ζ (zeta). Low damping means a sharp resonance peak; about 0.6 to 0.7 gives the flattest response.

Part of the damping is mechanical. The rest is electrical: if you connect a resistor across the coil, the coil's own voltage drives a current, and a current in a magnetic field gives a force that opposes the motion. A smaller resistor means more current and more damping. The datasheet values for the SM-24 datasheet:

Load across the coilDamping ζ
Open circuit (typical)0.25
1339 Ω calibration shunt (tolerance +5%, −0%)0.6
1000 Ω (read from the brochure's response-curve legend)0.69

The first rig puts a 1 kΩ shunt across the coil, which gives about 0.69. You can reproduce the datasheet values with a simple model calculated: electrical damping is (B·l)² / (2·m·ωn·(Rcoil + Rshunt)), and it adds to the open-circuit damping of 0.25.

B·l = 28.8 V/(m/s), m = 0.011 kg, ωn = 2π·10 = 62.83 rad/s, Rcoil = 375 Ω 1 kΩ shunt: (28.8)² / (2 · 0.011 · 62.83 · 1375) = 829.4 / 1900.8 = 0.436 → ζ = 0.25 + 0.436 = 0.69 1339 Ω shunt: 829.4 / (2 · 0.011 · 62.83 · 1714) = 829.4 / 2369 = 0.350 → ζ = 0.25 + 0.350 = 0.60

Both results match the datasheet. The model is the standard textbook one and is a good way to explain damping to a judge.

Reading the figure: with the 1 kΩ shunt (ζ = 0.69) there is no resonance peak, and the response is within 0.2 dB of flat from 20 Hz upward in this ideal model calculated. At 10 Hz it is 2.8 dB down (a factor of 0.72); at 5 Hz it is 12 dB down. Signals below 10 Hz are therefore weak, not absent.

Two things to settle on the rig

The shunt lowers the output. The 28.8 V/m/s figure is the generator constant. With a 1 kΩ shunt across a 375 Ω coil, the coil and the shunt form a divider, so the voltage the ADC sees is 1000 / (375 + 1000) = 0.727 of the open-circuit voltage: about 20.9 V/m/s calculated. (The ADC's input impedance of at least 1 MΩ at gain 32 does not load it noticeably.) The head descriptor in the repo lists 28.8, and it has a separate field for the shunt value, so the firmware or the analysis must correct for it.

The pack low-pass is above the datasheet's spurious frequency. The manifests start at a 400 Hz low-pass; the SM-24's typical spurious frequency is above 240 Hz. This is only a starting guess, but check what the recordings show above 240 Hz.

Worked example: floor velocity to counts at the ADC (gain 32)

Take a peak floor velocity of 100 µm/s assumed; it is only an example, and the first recordings will give the real order of magnitude.

Effective sensitivity with 1 kΩ shunt ≈ 20.9 V per m/s Voltage = 20.9 · 100·10⁻⁶ = 2.09 mV (open circuit would be 2.88 mV) Gain 32: full scale ±37.5 mV, 1 LSB = 4.47 nV Counts = 2.09 mV / 4.47 nV ≈ 468 500 LSB = 5.6% of full scale

A very small floor motion of 1 µm/s gives 20.9 µV, which is about 4 700 LSB. The ADC's own noise at gain 32 and 1 kSPS is 0.77 µV rms datasheet, about 172 LSB, so this small signal is still about 27 times the noise.

The strongest signal this setting can hold: ±37.5 mV / 20.9 V per m/s = 1.79 mm/s calculated. A harder stomp near the sensor than that clips. If it does, lower the gain to 16 (full scale ±75 mV). The whole chain noise expressed as floor velocity: 0.77 µV / 20.9 V per m/s ≈ 37 nm/s rms (the geophone's own thermal noise is far smaller: a 375 Ω coil gives about 2.5 nV/√Hz, around 25 nV over 100 Hz, calculated).

4bADXL355 accelerometer

The ADXL355 is a 3-axis MEMS accelerometer with a 20-bit digital output. Ranges: ±2 g, ±4 g and ±8 g. Noise density: 25 µg/√Hz (X, Y, Z). Current in measurement mode: about 200 µA. Interfaces: SPI and I²C. Analog Devices (the datasheet host was not reachable when this guide was written; these are the only ADXL355 facts used. Noise density: datasheet Rev. 0, 9/2016, p.3; later revisions may differ. Current: Rev. 0, p.4–5.)

How a MEMS accelerometer works

MEMS stands for micro-electro-mechanical system: a tiny mechanical structure etched in silicon. A small proof mass hangs on silicon springs between fixed electrodes. When the chip accelerates, the mass lags and moves relative to the frame. The mass and the electrodes form capacitors; the movement changes the capacitances. Electronics measure that change and an internal converter turns it into a digital number. Unlike the geophone, a MEMS accelerometer responds all the way down to a constant acceleration, so it also senses gravity and therefore tilt.

Why it fits machines

  • It is mounted on the machine. A motor or pump has a rigid housing, and the accelerometer sits on it (a magnet mount in the head design). It measures the housing's acceleration directly.
  • Machine vibration is periodic and rich in higher frequencies. The shaft speed and its harmonics, bearings and imbalance all appear as lines in a spectrum. Acceleration grows with frequency (a = 2π f v), so it emphasises exactly these components.
  • It is digital. The signal never travels as a small analog voltage, so cable pickup is not a problem and it needs no ADC channel.

What "noise density 25 µg/√Hz" means

Noise density is the noise per square root of bandwidth. The total rms noise is the density times the square root of the bandwidth you actually use:

noiserms = density · √bandwidth

Worked example: ADXL355 noise over a 100 Hz band

25 µg/√Hz · √100 Hz = 25 · 10 = 250 µg rms 250 µg · 9.80665 µm/s² per µg = 2.5 mm/s² rms

Now compare with the geophone for a floor signal (section 3): 1 µm/s at 20 Hz is only 12.8 µg of acceleration, about 19.5 times below that noise level. The same 1 µm/s gives the geophone 20.9 µV, 27 times above the ADC noise. Put the other way, the accelerometer's noise corresponds to a velocity of 250 µg · 9.80665 / (2π · 20) ≈ 19.5 µm/s at 20 Hz calculated, hundreds of times more than the geophone chain's noise (37 nm/s). The bandwidths differ, so the ratio is only an order of magnitude. The conclusion holds: for small, low-frequency floor motion the geophone is far more sensitive. For a running machine with large accelerations, the accelerometer is fine.

Wiring the ADXL355 for I²C Analog Devices

On the ADXL355 chip, four pins are shared between SPI and I²C: pin 1 is CS (SPI) or SCL (I²C); pin 2 is SCLK (SPI) or VSSIO (for I²C, connect it to VSSIO); pin 3 is MOSI (SPI) or SDA (I²C); pin 4 is MISO (SPI) or ASEL (I²C address select). The chip detects SPI or I²C automatically, so there is no separate mode pin. The I²C address is 0x1D with ASEL low and 0x53 with ASEL high (the repo's example descriptor uses 29, which is 0x1D). The bus can run at 100 kHz, 400 kHz, 1 MHz or 3.4 MHz; the ESP32-S3 side supports up to 400 kbit/s (and about 800 kbit/s with strong pull-ups), so use 100 or 400 kHz. External pull-up resistors on SDA and SCL are needed.

4cPiezo disc (later)

A piezoelectric disc, the kind used in buzzers (the head design uses a 27 to 35 mm brass disc), makes an electric charge when it bends. It responds to strain on the surface it is glued to. It is cheap and sensitive to impacts, which makes it interesting for later heads. It is not part of the first rig, because it needs electronics that the first rig does not have.

Why a buffer and a clamp are mandatory:

  • The disc is a high-impedance source. Electrically it is a small capacitor that generates a voltage. If the following circuit draws any current, the signal sags. The ADS131M04's differential input impedance is 330 kΩ at gains 1, 2 and 4, and at least 1 MΩ at gains 8 to 128 datasheet. Together with the disc's capacitance, that input resistance forms a high-pass filter. For an assumed disc capacitance of 20 nF assumed and 330 kΩ, the corner is 1/(2π · 330 kΩ · 20 nF) ≈ 24 Hz calculated, which would cut low-frequency content. A buffer amplifier (a unity-gain, very high-input-impedance stage) prevents this and gives the ADC a low-impedance signal.
  • A hard hit makes a big voltage. The disc can produce voltages far above what the ADC survives. The ADS131M04 absolute maximum on any analog input is AGND − 1.6 V to AVDD + 0.3 V datasheet. Going outside can damage the chip permanently. Clamp diodes limit the voltage before it reaches the ADC.

Clamping is not as simple as two diodes at the ADC pin

The ADS131M04 has a negative charge pump so that inputs can go below ground. For that reason, the datasheet says shunt diodes between the inputs and AGND cannot be used to clamp excessive negative voltages. The clamp has to be designed with this in mind, in the buffer stage, with a series resistor limiting current into the ADC pin. The exact circuit is a PCB-phase design task and is not decided.

Say it to a judge

We use a geophone on floors because it outputs velocity, and for small low-frequency floor motion velocity gives far more signal than acceleration does. We use a MEMS accelerometer on machines because it mounts directly on the housing and sees the higher-frequency content that identifies imbalance and bearing wear. Both are standard sensors with published datasheets.

Self-check

1. What does a geophone's output voltage depend on?

On the velocity of the coil relative to the magnet (e = B·l·v). With a 10 Hz natural frequency, above about 10 Hz that equals the floor velocity. The sensitivity is 28.8 V per m/s.

2. Why does a 1 kΩ shunt across the coil change the response?

It lets the coil's voltage drive a current. The current in the magnetic field produces a force opposing the motion, which adds electrical damping. The shunt takes the damping from 0.25 (open) to about 0.69 and removes the resonance peak. It also forms a divider with the 375 Ω coil, so the output is about 0.73 of the open-circuit value.

3. What is the shape of the response below the natural frequency?

It rolls off at about 12 dB per octave; the output falls toward zero as the frequency goes down.

4. A sensor has noise density 25 µg/√Hz. What is the noise in a 400 Hz band?

25 · √400 = 25 · 20 = 500 µg rms.

5. Why must a piezo head have a buffer and a clamp?

The disc is a high-impedance source that would be loaded by the ADC input, and it can produce voltages beyond the ADC's absolute maximum (AGND − 1.6 V to AVDD + 0.3 V). The buffer fixes the impedance; the clamp protects the input.

From voltage to numbers: the ADS131M04

An analog-to-digital converter measures a voltage many times per second and reports each measurement as an integer. The ADS131M04 does this for four channels at the same instant, with 24-bit results. This section covers the general ideas first and then the exact behaviour of this chip.

Sampling and the Nyquist limit

An ADC does not see a continuous signal. It takes samples: one measurement every 1/fs seconds, where fs is the sample rate. The Nyquist limit is fs/2: a signal can only be represented correctly if it contains no frequency above fs/2. At fs = 1000 samples per second (1 kSPS, the footstep-id starting rate repo), the Nyquist limit is 500 Hz.

A frequency above the limit does not disappear. It folds back and appears as a lower frequency that was never in the original signal. This is aliasing. For a frequency f between fs/2 and fs, the alias appears at fs − f.

Aliasing cannot be repaired after sampling. It must be prevented before, by removing frequencies above the Nyquist limit with a low-pass filter. In the ADS131M04 a digital filter inside the chip (next sections) does part of this job, but it is weak close to Nyquist, and the analog RC filter at the inputs protects against tones near the modulator clock, not near Nyquist (see the antialiasing box below).

Resolution and LSB size

An N-bit converter has 2N possible output codes. It spreads its input range (the full-scale range, FSR) over those codes. The voltage step between two neighbouring codes is the LSB (least significant bit):

LSB = (total input span) / 2N

The ADS131M04 gives 24-bit results in two's complement: 000000h is zero, 7FFFFFh is the most positive code, 800000h the most negative. With 24 bits there are 16 777 216 codes. The datasheet formula is datasheet

1 LSB = (2.4 V / Gain) / 2²⁴ = FSR / 2²³,  FSR = 1.2 V / Gain

Resolution is not accuracy. The bottom bits of a 24-bit result are lost in noise. What counts is the dynamic range: the ratio between the largest signal and the noise, in dB. The datasheet turns noise into an "effective resolution" in bits (Table 7-2). At gain 32 and 1 kSPS it lists a dynamic range of 91 dB, equivalent to 16.6 effective bits datasheet. That is still very good: 91 dB is a factor of about 35 000 between full scale and noise.

Delta-sigma conversion

The ADS131M04 is a delta-sigma (ΔΣ) converter. It works in two stages and trades speed for precision.

  1. A fast, crude modulator. A feedback loop measures the input with a one-bit comparator, far faster than the final sample rate. The modulator frequency is fMOD = fCLKIN / 2 = 4.096 MHz with an 8.192 MHz clock datasheet. The loop subtracts a feedback value, integrates the difference and quantises it to one bit. The output is a bit-stream whose density of ones follows the input voltage (the datasheet calls it a "one's density modulated bit-stream").
  2. A digital filter that averages and decimates. A digital low-pass filter (sinc-type, linear phase) averages many bits to produce one accurate 24-bit word, and keeps only one word out of every OSR bits. The decimation factor is the oversampling ratio: OSR = fMOD / fDATA datasheet.

Two ideas explain why this gives high resolution. Oversampling spreads the same quantisation noise over a wider frequency range, so less of it lands in the band you care about. Noise shaping goes further: the feedback loop pushes the noise away from low frequencies toward high ones. The digital filter then removes most of that high-frequency noise. A higher OSR means more averaging, lower bandwidth and lower noise. Halving the data rate lowers the noise (Table 5.1).

ADS131M04 in detail

Four channels at the same instant

The chip has four differential inputs (AIN0P/N to AIN3P/N). Each channel has its own PGA, its own modulator and its own digital filter. All run from the same clock, so all four channels are sampled at the same instant, and they appear together in one SPI frame datasheet. (By default there is no delay between channels. The chip can add a programmable per-channel phase delay in steps of 244 ns at 8.192 MHz, used to compensate known sensor delays.)

Why simultaneity matters:

  • Comparing sensors. If two geophones are read one after the other, the same wave appears at slightly different times and the amplitudes belong to different moments. With simultaneous sampling, any difference between channels is a real difference in the floor, not an artefact of the converter.
  • Localisation (later). To find where a step happened you compare the arrival time of the wave at several sensors. Time differences are small; a converter that samples channels one after another would add its own timing offset. Multiplexed converters (for example the ADS1256) convert one channel at a time.

The Year 1 manifests set simultaneous: false, so no Year 1 pack relies on it yet repo. The chip is chosen for the later packs; the first rig records two geophones at the same instant so the data can be used that way.

Programmable gain amplifier and full-scale range

Each channel has a PGA with gains 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64 and 128. The gain scales the full-scale range: FSR = ±1.2 V / gain, from the internal 1.2 V reference datasheet. A higher gain means a smaller range, finer steps and, as a ratio, a better view of small signals.

The clock: why an external 8.192 MHz

The ADS131M04 has no internal oscillator. It needs a continuous LVCMOS clock on CLKIN whenever it runs datasheet. Because the ADC's sampling clock is the oscillator, the sample rate is only as accurate and stable as the oscillator. The QTM325 oscillator is specified at ±50 ppm or ±25 ppm (options) over temperature, supply, load and first-year aging, with 1 ps rms maximum phase jitter and a start-up time of 5 ms maximum datasheet. ±50 ppm means a clock error of at most 4.3 s per day calculated. The datasheet also asks for a clock free of overshoot and glitches, because glitches on CLKIN add noise to the data. The ESP32-S3 could generate such a clock, but a dedicated oscillator is independent of the CPU and its firmware, and that is the cleaner choice.

Startup and reset

After the supplies reach 90% of nominal, the power-on reset takes 250 µs. A low-to-high edge on DRDY then says the SPI interface is ready, and the chip ignores any SPI traffic before it. First data arrives within 0.5 ms of the supply ramp datasheet. A reset (by pin, by command, or at power-up) sets every register to its default: 24-bit words, input CRC off, OSR 1024 (4 kSPS), all four channels enabled, gain 1.

The SPI frame

All communication happens in fixed frames. With the default 24-bit word size, a frame is six words, 144 SCLK cycles datasheet:

  1. Word 0 on DOUT: the response to the command sent in the previous frame. When the host sends NULL (0x0000), the response is the STATUS register: reset flag, CRC error flag, word length, a flag that shows a resynchronisation happened, and one data-ready bit per channel.
  2. Words 1 to 4: conversion data of channels 0 to 3, 24 bits each in two's complement.
  3. Word 5: a 16-bit CRC (padded to the word size).

On DIN the host sends a command word, a CRC word (or zeros when input CRC is off), and four words of zeros. The word size can be changed to 16 bits (the ADC truncates data to 16 bits) or 32 bits (zero padded or sign extended) in the MODE register. Commands, responses, CRC and register values always carry 16 bits of real data, left-aligned.

CRC is an error-check code. The CRC polynomial is CCITT by default (x16 + x12 + x5 + 1) with seed FFFFh. The ADC always appends an output CRC; the host may check it to catch bit errors. Input CRC on DIN is optional and off by default. Use it for register writes (a WREG command executes even if its input CRC fails, and sets a flag).

Two behaviours to remember for the firmware:

  • The ADC keeps a small FIFO of two samples per channel. If the host misses sample sets, collect data again after a gap by reading two frames in a row, or by pulsing SYNC/RESET to clear the FIFOs.
  • DRDY goes low when new data is ready and stays low until the data is read (or briefly pulses high before the next one). Read inside the sample period. Avoid reading while the next conversion completes.

To check a first wiring, read the ID register: send RREG for address 0 (0xA000); the response word should read 0x24xx. The upper four bits read 0010b and the next four (the channel count) read 0100b datasheet. After a RESET command the ADC acknowledges with 0xFF24.

The footstep-id pack currently says 1000 Hz, so this is an open decision for the team. The alternative is to keep 1 kSPS and write the aliasing risk down as a known limitation.

Self-check

1. At 1 kSPS, what is the Nyquist frequency, and where does a 700 Hz tone show up after sampling?

Nyquist is 500 Hz. 700 Hz is above it, so it aliases to 1000 − 700 = 300 Hz.

2. Compute the full-scale range and LSB at gain 16.

FSR = ±1.2 V / 16 = ±75 mV. LSB = 150 mV / 2²⁴ = 8.94 nV.

3. What is OSR and how does it relate to the data rate?

OSR = fMOD / fDATA, the decimation factor of the digital filter. With fMOD = 4.096 MHz, OSR 1024 gives 4 kSPS and OSR 4096 gives 1 kSPS. A higher OSR means a lower data rate and lower noise.

5. How many SCLK cycles does one frame take in the default word size, and what is in it?

144 (six words of 24 bits): a response or STATUS word, four channel words, one CRC word.

How the chips talk: SPI and I²C

Chips exchange data over short digital buses. The prototype uses two: SPI for the fast, steady stream (the ADC and the SD card) and I²C for slow, occasional conversations (head EEPROMs and the ADXL355).

SPI

SPI (Serial Peripheral Interface) connects one controller (the ESP32-S3) to one or more peripherals. It uses four signals:

SignalAlso calledDirectionJob
SCLKclockcontroller → peripheralEvery clock cycle moves one bit.
MOSIDIN on the ADS131M04controller → peripheralData from the controller.
MISODOUT on the ADS131M04peripheral → controllerData to the controller.
CSchip select, active lowcontroller → peripheralSelects the peripheral. When CS is high, the peripheral ignores the bus and releases MISO.

SPI is full duplex: on every clock cycle a bit goes out on MOSI and a bit comes in on MISO, at the same time. Several peripherals can share SCLK, MOSI and MISO if each has its own CS line.

Clock polarity and phase: which SPI mode

Two settings must match on both sides. CPOL is the idle level of the clock (0 = low). CPHA says which clock edge moves data and which captures it. The two bits give four modes, numbered 0 to 3. The ADS131M04 uses SPI mode 1: CPOL = 0, CPHA = 1. The clock idles low; the sender changes data on rising edges; both sides capture data on falling edges datasheet. If the ESP32-S3 is set to the wrong mode, the data looks plausible but is shifted by one bit. This is a common reason for a wrong ID-register readout.

Timing limits for DVDD of 2.7 V to 3.6 V datasheet: SCLK period at least 40 ns (so at most 25 MHz); the first SCLK rising edge at least 16 ns after CS falls; CS high for at least 15 ns between frames; at least 10 ns between the last SCLK falling edge and CS rising. The ESP32-S3's general-purpose SPI controller supports full-duplex clocks up to 80 MHz datasheet, so the ADC sets the limit.

DRDY, the interrupt and the read

The ESP32-S3 should not poll the ADC. The ADC tells it when a sample set is ready using the DRDY pin:

  1. The ADC finishes a conversion on all channels and pulls DRDY low.
  2. The ESP32-S3 sees the falling edge on a GPIO and an interrupt service routine (ISR) runs. It does the minimum: record a timestamp and wake the acquire task.
  3. The acquire task starts an SPI transaction with DMA (direct memory access): the SPI hardware clocks 144 bits and stores the received bytes in memory without the CPU copying them.
  4. When the transfer finishes, the task unpacks the four 24-bit channel words, checks the status word and the CRC, adds a sequence number and writes the samples into the ring buffer (section 7).
  5. Back to step 1, one sample period later.

Worked example: how busy is the bus?

Frame = 6 words × 24 bits = 144 SCLK cycles At SCLK = 8 MHz (chosen for the example): 144 / 8 MHz = 18 µs At 1 kSPS the sample period is 1000 µs → the bus is busy 1.8% of the time At 4 kSPS (250 µs): 7.2%. At 64 kSPS (15.6 µs): 144 bits need at least 9.2 MHz of continuous clock

At 1 kSPS there is plenty of slack. Near 64 kSPS there is almost none, which is one reason very high rates are used only for short event clips, while continuous recording stays at 4 kSPS or below.

I²C

I²C (Inter-Integrated Circuit; Analog Devices writes it I²C with a superscript 2) needs only two wires for any number of devices: SDA (data) and SCL (clock). It is slower than SPI but cheap in wiring, which suits things that are read rarely or at modest speed.

  • Open-drain with pull-ups. No device ever drives a line high. Devices only pull a line low or let go. A pull-up resistor to 3.3 V on each line makes the high level. Because nobody drives high, two devices can never fight each other. Typical pull-up values are a few kΩ; the right value depends on the bus capacitance and the speed.
  • Addresses. Every device has a 7-bit address. The controller starts a transaction by sending the address of the device it wants, plus one bit for read or write.
  • Start and stop. A start is SDA falling while SCL is high. A stop is SDA rising while SCL is high. At all other times SDA may only change while SCL is low.
  • Acknowledge. After each 8-bit byte, the receiver pulls SDA low for one clock to say "received". A high on the ninth clock (no ACK) means nobody answered or the receiver refused.

Speeds on the ESP32-S3 datasheet: standard mode 100 kbit/s, fast mode 400 kbit/s, and up to 800 kbit/s depending on the strength of the pull-ups. The ESP32-S3 has two I²C controllers.

Why the ADC is on SPI and the others on I²C

  • The ADS131M04 only has SPI. Its datasheet describes an SPI-compatible interface and nothing else.
  • It also needs the speed. One frame is 144 bits. At 1 kSPS that is 144 kbit/s of raw bits; at 4 kSPS 576 kbit/s, more than I²C fast mode (400 kbit/s) can carry, before counting I²C's extra acknowledge bits.
  • EEPROMs and the ADXL355 are slow and sparse. A head's identity is read once at boot. The ADXL355 at the appliance pack's 2000 Hz produces 3 axes × 3 B × 2000 = 18 kB/s before I²C overhead calculated (packs/appliance/pack.json sets sample_rate_hz 2000). I²C's two wires pass through an RJ45 head cable cheaply, and many parts share them.
  • Open question: does 2000 Hz fit over I²C? A search snippet (unverified) says the maximum output data rate at 400 kHz I²C is about 800 Hz. Whether the team uses 1 MHz Fast-mode Plus or 3.4 MHz, lowers the data rate, or moves the ADXL355 to SPI is still to be checked and decided.

Why identical EEPROM addresses need the TCA9548A mux

Every head carries a small EEPROM (an AT24C02, 2 kbit) that holds its descriptor: what it is, its serial number, its sensitivity. That EEPROM family has a fixed address block starting at 0x50, with the exact address set by three pins typical, from the AT24C02 family datasheet, which is not in this guide's source set. Heads are meant to be plug-and-play and identical in design, so all heads will answer at the same address. Four heads on one bus would collide: the controller cannot say which one it is talking to.

The fix is an I²C multiplexer, the TCA9548A (an 8-channel I²C switch; it has its own address, typically 0x70 to 0x77 by pin typical, from the TI datasheet). The controller first writes to the mux which downstream port to connect, then talks to the EEPROM on that port. The other ports are disconnected. The same address is then used safely on every port, and each head's cable capacitance is kept off the main bus.

The ADC streams data over SPI, which is fast and full duplex, and signals each new sample with a data-ready pin that triggers an interrupt, so every sample is read or counted as missed. Self-check

1. Which SPI mode does the ADS131M04 use, and what does that mean for the clock?

Mode 1: CPOL = 0, CPHA = 1. The clock idles low, data changes on rising edges, and both sides read it on falling edges.

2. Walk through what happens between DRDY falling and the samples being in the ring buffer.

GPIO interrupt: timestamp and wake the acquire task. The task starts an SPI DMA transfer of the 144-bit frame, then unpacks the four 24-bit words, checks status and CRC, adds a sequence number and writes into the buffer.

3. Why does I²C need pull-up resistors?

The lines are open-drain: devices can only pull them low. The resistors pull them back high when nobody is pulling.

4. Two heads have an EEPROM at the same address. How do you read both?

Put an I²C multiplexer (TCA9548A) between the ESP32-S3 and the heads. Select port 1, read; select port 2, read. Only one head is connected to the bus at a time.

5. Why is the head port labelled "not Ethernet"?

It uses an RJ45 jack and cable but carries analog signals, I²C and 3.3 V on different pins than Ethernet does. Ethernet or PoE equipment plugged in could put voltage on pins connected to the analog input and I²C.

What N16R8 means

The board name ESP32-S3-DEV-N16R8 tells you the memory: N16 is 16 MB of flash (program and file storage), R8 is 8 MB of PSRAM (extra RAM). The PSRAM is octal: it talks to the chip over eight data lines. The ESP32-S3 datasheet lists chips such as the ESP32-S3R8 with 8 MB of octal-SPI in-package PSRAM datasheet.

Why 8 MB of RAM? The internal 512 KB would hold under a second of data at the highest rates, and also needs room for the tasks, Wi-Fi and the model. PSRAM holds the sample ring buffer, event windows and the machine-learning working memory. PSRAM is reached through a cache and is slower than internal SRAM, so latency-critical code and DMA buffers use internal RAM, and bulk storage uses PSRAM.

When you first flash the firmware, it prints the flash and PSRAM size. If it says PSRAM is missing, the board is not an N16R8 variant repo.

Temperature note: chips with octal PSRAM are specified for an ambient range of −40 °C to 65 °C, or up to 85 °C with the PSRAM error-correction option enabled, which reduces the usable PSRAM by 1/16 datasheet. Fine for indoor use; remember it for an enclosure near a heat source.

Why GPIO 33 to 37 are taken

In-package flash and PSRAM are wired to the chip through dedicated pins. With octal PSRAM, the chip uses GPIO 33, 34, 35 and 36 as data lines 4 to 7 and GPIO 37 as the data strobe (DQS) for the PSRAM (ESP32-S3 datasheet, Table 2-14) datasheet. They are not free to use. Assigning them to something else breaks the PSRAM, usually with a crash at boot. The rule: never assign GPIO 33 to 37.

The chip datasheet lists GPIO 33 to 37

The chip datasheet (Table 2-14) lists GPIO 33 and 34 as well (data lines 4 and 5) for octal SPI flash or PSRAM. The repo's pin note mentions only 35 to 37; that note is being corrected to 33 to 37. Dev boards often do not expose 33 and 34, but check your board's pinout before using them.

Other pins that need care datasheet: GPIO 0, 3, 45 and 46 are strapping pins (their level at reset selects boot options); GPIO 19 and 20 are the USB pins; GPIO 39 to 42 are the JTAG pins; GPIO 43 and 44 are UART0 (the serial console).

DMA and interrupts

Interrupt. A hardware event (here: DRDY falling) makes the CPU stop what it is doing, run a short function (the ISR), and return. Interrupts give you timing you cannot get by polling. Rules for an ISR: keep it very short, do not block, do not write to the SD card, do not use floating point at all (on the ESP32-S3 with ESP-IDF, floating point in an ISR is not allowed). It should only timestamp the event and signal a task.

DMA (direct memory access) lets a peripheral move data to or from memory without the CPU. For the ADC: the CPU starts the SPI transfer; the SPI controller and the GDMA clock 18 bytes into a buffer; the CPU is notified when it is done. During the transfer, the CPU is free.

FreeRTOS tasks

The firmware runs under FreeRTOS, a small real-time operating system included with ESP-IDF. A task is a function with its own stack that runs "at the same time" as others; the scheduler gives each task the CPU according to its priority. Tasks communicate through queues, semaphores and task notifications. In ESP-IDF on a dual-core chip, you can pin a task to a core.

The planned components in the repo map onto tasks repo: acquire (ADC driver), storage (WAV and sidecar writer), trigger, features, engines, net. The principle: the acquire path has the highest priority and is never blocked by slow work. SD cards sometimes stall for a long time while they do internal housekeeping; Wi-Fi bursts take CPU time. The ring buffer gives the slow tasks room to catch up.

The PSRAM ring buffer

A ring buffer is a fixed block of memory used as a circle. A write position moves forward as new samples arrive; when it reaches the end it wraps around to the start and overwrites the oldest data. Each reader (storage, trigger and so on) has its own read position. This gives two things the packs need:

  • History. When a trigger fires, the data from before the trigger (the footstep-id pack keeps 100 ms before, the fall pack 500 ms) is still in the buffer.
  • Decoupling. The acquire task writes at a fixed rate. Readers take what they need at their own pace, as long as none falls behind by more than the buffer length.

Each stored sample set carries a sequence number and a timestamp from the DRDY interrupt, so any gap can be detected later.

Count every sample

The project's rule: firmware reports dropped samples and never hides them repo. In practice that means a counter of samples that were produced by the ADC but did not reach the buffer or the file. Gaps are detected by several mechanisms:

  • the sequence number: a jump means samples were lost;
  • the ADC's STATUS word: the resynchronisation flag and the per-channel data-ready flags show missed reads;
  • the CRC: a failed check means the frame is corrupt;
  • a full ring buffer: the overwrite of data a slow reader has not read yet is counted.

Why this matters: for a machine-learning dataset, a hidden gap looks like a pattern in the data. A counted gap can be handled. The sidecar schema (section 9) has no dedicated field for the dropped-sample count yet; where it is reported is not decided.

Worked example: how much data is that?

The recording format stores 32-bit integer samples (4 bytes) for every channel repo.

The point: the buffer holds minutes, far more than the longest card stall, and storage throughput is small compared with what an SD card can write. The data rate is not the problem. Timing and gaps are.

Power and clean analog

The signals are microvolts. Anything that adds even a few microvolts of noise to the supply, the ground or the clock shows up in the data. Clean analog design here means: separate the quiet from the noisy, give every chip a local energy reserve, and keep wires short.

AVDD, DVDD, AGND and DGND

The ADS131M04 has two supplies datasheet:

  • AVDD powers the analog parts: PGA, modulators, reference. Allowed 2.7 V to 3.6 V (3.0 V nominal).
  • DVDD powers the digital interface and, through an internal regulator, the digital core. Also 2.7 V to 3.6 V. It sets the logic level of the SPI lines, so with the ESP32-S3 at 3.3 V, DVDD is 3.3 V.
  • CAP is the output of that internal 1.8 V regulator. It is not a supply input; it needs a capacitor (220 nF to DGND when DVDD is above 2.7 V).
  • AGND and DGND are the matching grounds. The datasheet recommends connecting them together as close to the chip as possible. A single connection point avoids ground loops, in which digital return currents flow through the analog ground and add noise.

In the first rig both supplies come from the dev board's 3.3 V rail, which is within the allowed range. The analog supply goes through a small RC filter.

The 10 Ω + 10 µF filter

A resistor in series with a capacitor to ground forms a first-order low-pass filter. It lets steady current through and blocks fast changes in the supply voltage from reaching AVDD. The cut-off frequency is where the filter has cut the signal by 3 dB:

fc = 1 / (2π · R · C) R = 10 Ω, C = 10 µF → R·C = 100 µs → fc = 1 / (2π · 0.0001 s) = 1 592 Hz

Worked example: what the filter does and does not do

  • Noise above 1.6 kHz is attenuated at 20 dB per decade. At 100 kHz the attenuation is about 20·log10(100 000 / 1 592) ≈ 36 dB calculated. This helps against fast switching noise from the digital side and from the dev board's regulator.
  • Noise below 1.6 kHz is not filtered. Supply ripple at 50 Hz mains harmonics or at the frequencies of Wi-Fi activity reaches AVDD. The ADC's own rejection of supply noise takes over there: PSRR on AVDD is 75 dB at DC and 78 dB at 50 Hz or 60 Hz datasheet.
  • The resistor drops voltage. At the typical analog supply current of 3.5 mA the drop is 10 Ω × 3.5 mA = 35 mV; at the 4.0 mA maximum, 40 mV datasheet calculated. The AVDD from a 3.3 V rail is then about 3.26 V, inside the 2.7 V to 3.6 V range.

A larger R·C pushes the corner lower, but a larger R drops more voltage, and the capacitor has to be a type that is stable: ceramic capacitors lose capacitance at DC bias, so the real value of a 10 µF ceramic capacitor can be considerably lower than 10 µF. Choose the part with this in mind or check it on the bench.

Decoupling capacitors: why close to the pin

Digital circuits draw current in short spikes when they switch. The supply wire has inductance, so it cannot deliver a spike instantly; instead the voltage at the chip dips. A capacitor right at the pin holds a small reserve of charge and supplies the spike locally. Its impedance falls with frequency: XC = 1 / (2π f C). At 1 MHz a 100 nF capacitor is 1.6 Ω and a 1 µF capacitor is 0.16 Ω calculated. Every extra millimetre of lead adds inductance (about 1 nH per millimetre of wire is a common rule of thumb approximate), so a capacitor that is far from the pin is much less effective at high frequencies than its capacitance suggests.

The first-rig plan says 100 nF; the datasheet asks for more

The repo plan gives "100 nF on every supply pin". The datasheet says AVDD and DVDD must each be decoupled with a 1 µF capacitor, and the CAP pin needs 220 nF, all as close to the pins as possible (Section 10). Follow the datasheet: 1 µF on AVDD, 1 µF on DVDD, 220 nF on CAP. A 100 nF ceramic capacitor in parallel next to each is harmless and helps at high frequency. Use ceramic X7R capacitors for supply decoupling; the datasheet says Y5V is not recommended, and that C0G capacitors suit the analog inputs.

Why a low-noise LDO comes on the PCB

On the breadboard, AVDD is a filtered version of the dev board's 3.3 V. That rail is made from the 5 V USB supply by a regulator that is shared with Wi-Fi and everything else on the board. Wi-Fi transmit bursts pull large current in short pulses, which makes the rail move. An RC filter helps above 1.6 kHz but cannot fix what happens below it. On the PCB, a separate low-noise LDO (low-dropout linear regulator) will make a clean AVDD of its own. This is a planned change, not a first-rig item.

Ground loops and the limits of a breadboard

  • Ground loop: if two ground paths connect the same two points, the loop between them picks up magnetic interference like an antenna, and shared return currents create voltage differences. Use one connection point (a "star") between the analog and the digital ground.
  • Breadboard contacts have variable resistance, and the long, loose wires are antennas for interference. The strips add capacitance between neighbouring rows.
  • The 8.192 MHz clock has fast edges (the QTM325 specifies rise and fall times of 8 ns maximum datasheet), which radiate and couple into nearby analog wires. Keep the CLKIN wire short, put the oscillator close to the ADC, and keep it away from the geophone inputs. The ADC datasheet advises that a source-termination resistor at the clock source often helps reduce overshoot; a small series resistor at the oscillator output is a cheap thing to try.
  • SPI lines toggle too. Keep them away from the analog inputs.
  • The geophone cables carry microvolts. Use a twisted pair, as short as you can, and tie AINxN to AGND at the ADC.

The breadboard is for learning and for the first recordings. The datasheet's layout advice (a dedicated ground plane, digital traces routed away from analog inputs, capacitors close to the pins) can only be followed properly on the PCB. If the first recordings show more noise than the datasheet predicts (0.77 µV rms at gain 32 and 1 kSPS), the breadboard is the first suspect.

Say it to a judge

The signals are microvolts, so we treat supply and ground as part of the measurement. On the breadboard AVDD is filtered by 10 ohms and 10 microfarads, with a corner at about 1.6 kilohertz, and every supply pin has local decoupling as the datasheet specifies. Storage and data format

A recording is only useful years later if it says exactly how it was made. The project stores every recording as two files with the same base name: a WAV file with the samples and a JSON sidecar with the setup and the labels.

The microSD card

The card is connected over SPI (the first rig) and holds a normal file system. A few practical points:

  • Throughput is not the problem. Four channels at 1 kSPS is 16 kB/s (section 7). Even the slowest card writes much faster.
  • Latency is the problem. A card occasionally stalls for a long time while it manages its flash internally. The ring buffer absorbs these pauses, and the storage task writes in large chunks rather than one sample at a time.
  • High-endurance cards. A node writes continuously, day and night. Flash memory wears out with writes. Cards sold as "high endurance" (made for dash cameras and security cameras) are designed for constant recording and are the right class for this job.
  • Close files properly. A WAV header holds the length of the file, which is only known at the end. If power is cut before the header is updated, the file may need repair. Writing the header again at regular intervals limits the damage.

WAV is a simple container for uncompressed samples. It is read by Audacity, Sonic Visualiser, Python (soundfile, SciPy) and almost every audio tool, which is why it was chosen: the dataset has to last three years and be readable by anyone. The project format is a multichannel WAV with 32-bit integer samples; continuous recording runs up to 4 kSPS, and shorter event clips are used above that repo.

The recording protocol for footstep-id

The protocol walk-pass-v1 defines how the person-ID dataset is recorded repo:

  • Written consent from everyone recorded, and a pseudonym (P1, P2, …) for each. The list of names stays off the repo.
  • Sensor positions measured in metres and written into the sidecar. Sixty seconds of silence recorded at the start of every session.
  • Per person and per floor: 50 normal-pace passes with shoes, 20 normal-pace without shoes, 10 slow with shoes, 10 fast with shoes: 90 passes. Each pass is a walk along a marked 4 m line past the sensors, then a stop and a 3 s wait.

  • At least two floor types across the dataset. Never test and train on the same day's recordings: evaluation splits by session.

  • What does go in git: the sidecars, the manifests that list the files, the schemas and the code. They describe the dataset without containing the sensitive part.

Signal processing

Raw samples are not yet decisions. This section covers the steps between the two: look at the signal in time and in frequency, remove what you do not need, find the events, and turn each event into a small set of numbers.

Time domain and frequency domain

The same signal can be shown two ways. In the time domain you plot amplitude against time: you see when something happened and how strong it was. In the frequency domain you plot how much of each frequency is present: you see what it is made of. A 50 Hz motor hum is a smear in the time domain but a sharp line at 50 Hz in the frequency domain. A footstep is a short burst in time and a broad hump in frequency.

FFT and spectrogram

The DFT (discrete Fourier transform) converts N samples into N/2 + 1 frequency bins; the FFT is the fast algorithm for it. The bins are spaced fs / N apart. For N = 1024 samples at 1 kSPS the spacing is 0.977 Hz and the highest bin is at 500 Hz, the Nyquist frequency calculated. Longer windows give finer frequency resolution but blur events in time.

Before the FFT you normally multiply the window by a smooth taper (for example a Hann window). Without it, the sharp edges of the cut-out segment smear every strong frequency across its neighbours ("spectral leakage").

A spectrogram is a series of short FFTs slid along the recording. Time runs across, frequency up, and brightness shows strength. It is the best way to look at footsteps and at machines whose sound changes over time.

Try it: when the first recording exists

Open a WAV file in Sonic Visualiser or Audacity and switch to a spectrogram view. Find a footstep. Is its energy at low or high frequencies? How long does it last? Then compute the same spectrogram in Python with scipy.signal.spectrogram, and check that the two pictures agree.

Filters

A filter passes some frequencies and attenuates others.

  • High-pass removes slow changes: constant offsets (DC), sensor drift, slow building sway. The footstep-id manifest starts at 5 Hz, fall at 2 Hz, appliance at 3 Hz repo. Remember that the SM-24 rolls off below its 10 Hz natural frequency anyway.
  • Low-pass removes fast noise and is needed before reducing the sample rate. The footstep-id and fall manifests start at 400 Hz.
  • Band-pass is the two together.

Two kinds of digital filter: FIR filters (finite impulse response) are always stable and can have exactly linear phase, but need many coefficients. IIR filters (for example Butterworth filters, usually built from a chain of second-order "biquad" stages) need very few coefficients and are cheap on a microcontroller, but they delay different frequencies by different amounts (phase distortion). In offline analysis a zero-phase function (scipy.signal.filtfilt) can cancel that; a real-time node cannot look into the future, so it uses a causal filter, and the Python code that mirrors it must use the same.

The ADS131M04 can do some of this itself: its decimation filter is a low-pass that follows the data rate (but only a gentle one near Nyquist, see section 5), and an optional DC-block high-pass is built in (section 5).

Decimation

Decimation by M reduces the sample rate by M: low-pass filter first so nothing is above the new Nyquist frequency, then keep every M-th sample. The ADC's own filter only does this weakly: at 1 kSPS it attenuates just 4.4 dB at Nyquist (section 5 and the antialiasing box). A sharp FIR in firmware does it properly. The pack manifest also has a decimate step for firmware-side reduction, for example from 4 kSPS to 1 kSPS (M = 4). Decimating without filtering first aliases the signal, as shown in section 5.

STA/LTA trigger

Recording everything and analysing everything is wasteful. A trigger decides when something interesting is happening. The classic way to detect a sudden burst in a seismic signal is STA/LTA: compare the recent average signal energy (short-term average, STA) with the long-run background (long-term average, LTA).

STA[n] = (1 / Ns) · Σ x[k]² over the last Ns samples LTA[n] = (1 / Nl) · Σ x[k]² over the last Nl samples ratio R[n] = STA[n] / LTA[n];  trigger when R[n] > threshold

With the footstep-id starting values repo at 1 kSPS: STA window 50 ms = 50 samples, LTA window 2000 ms = 2000 samples, threshold ratio 3.5, and the recording keeps 100 ms before and 400 ms after the trigger. In quiet conditions STA and LTA are about equal and R is near 1. When a burst arrives, STA jumps within a few tens of milliseconds while LTA barely moves.

Worked example: one burst

Background energy (the mean of x²) is 1 unit. A burst of 50 ms has energy 25 units assumed.

STA (50 samples, all inside the burst) = 25 LTA (2000 samples) = (1950 · 1 + 50 · 25) / 2000 = 3200 / 2000 = 1.6 R = 25 / 1.6 = 15.6 > 3.5 → trigger

The LTA moved only from 1.0 to 1.6, so the burst stands out. This is also why the LTA must be long: if it were as short as the STA the ratio could never exceed 1 by much.

Settings are a trade-off. A lower ratio catches weaker steps but also triggers on noise (false positives). A higher ratio misses soft steps. The 3.5 is a starting guess. The other triggers in the manifests are simpler: a fixed threshold on the signal level (the fall pack, level 0.0005, with 500 ms before and 3000 ms after), and periodic (the appliance pack: every 10 s). The unit of the threshold level is not specified in the manifest yet.

Footstep segmentation

Once a trigger fires, the signal around it is cut out as a window: for footstep-id from 100 ms before to 400 ms after, 500 samples at 1 kSPS. That window is one footstep, and it is what the feature code sees. Two refinements are needed in practice (neither is in the repo yet): a hold-off time so one footstep does not trigger twice, and a rule for the next step arriving before the window ends.

Features

A feature is a number computed from a window that describes it. The classifier does not see 500 raw samples; it sees maybe a few dozen features. Typical examples of vibration features typical examples: peak amplitude, energy, duration, the rise time of the onset, the decay rate of the ring-down, the energy in a handful of frequency bands, and the frequency at which the spectrum has its centre of mass. The manifests name three feature sets (footstep_v1, impact_v1, spectrum_bands_v1); their exact contents are not written yet.

One feature implementation, two languages

The model is trained in Python on features computed in Python. The node computes features in C or C++ on the ESP32-S3. If the two computations differ even slightly, the model sees inputs it was never trained on, and the accuracy measured in Python means nothing on the node. So the project has a rule: a Python feature change needs the matching firmware change and updated test vectors repo.

A test vector is a stored input with the expected output and a tolerance, for example ml/tests/vectors/<feature>.json. Both the Python code and the firmware must produce the stored output from the stored input within the tolerance. Use float32 in Python wherever the firmware uses float32, because float32 and float64 round differently. The feature code is Tier 1: it changes only together with an accepted decision.

Say it to a judge

We detect a footstep by comparing the energy of the last 50 milliseconds with the average of the last two seconds. When the ratio exceeds a threshold we cut out a window around it and compute features. The same feature code runs in Python for training and in C on the node, and shared test vectors prove they agree.

Self-check

1. What is the frequency spacing of a 2000-sample FFT at 1000 SPS?

fs / N = 1000 / 2000 = 0.5 Hz.

2. Why must you low-pass filter before decimating?

Frequencies above the new Nyquist limit would fold back (alias) into the band and cannot be removed afterwards.

3. With the starting values, how many samples are in the STA and LTA windows at 1 kSPS?

50 and 2000.

4. What happens to STA/LTA if the long window is made very short?

The LTA follows the burst almost as quickly as the STA, so the ratio stays near 1 and the trigger stops working.

5. Why is "one feature implementation, two languages" necessary, and how is it enforced?

The model is trained on Python features but runs on features from firmware; they must be identical. Test vectors (input, expected output, tolerance) shared by both implementations enforce it.

Machine learning for the packs

The models are small and the method is conservative: simple classifiers first, honest splits, a baseline next to every result, and an "unknown" answer for people the system has not met. Nothing here has been trained yet; this section explains the method and the pitfalls.

Footstep-id: enrolment, classifier, unknown

Enrolment. A person is "enrolled" by recording them walking according to the walk-pass protocol (section 9): many passes, with and without shoes, at three paces, on each floor. Each pass is labelled with a pseudonym. Footstep windows from those recordings become training examples.

Classifier. A classifier maps a feature vector to a label. The pack has labels P1, P2, P3 and unknown repo. Start with simple models: logistic regression, random forest, SVM (scikit-learn). A neural network is used only when a simple model plateaus and the data justifies it.

Unknown. A visitor is not in the training set. A classifier forced to choose among P1 to P3 will confidently name the wrong person. Two ways to reject: an explicit "unknown" class trained on other people's steps, or a confidence threshold (if the highest class probability is below a limit, answer "unknown"). Which one the project uses is to be decided by experiment.

Splitting by session

Data is divided into train (the model learns), validation (you tune settings) and test (the final grade, used once). The split must be by session or day, never by window. Windows from one walk are nearly identical; if some land in train and some in test, the model is graded on near-copies of what it has seen. The score looks excellent and tells you nothing about tomorrow. This is called leakage, and it is the most common way to fool yourself repo. Always report the split: which people and sessions were train, which were test.

Measuring accuracy

Accuracy is correct predictions divided by all predictions. It needs a reference: a baseline such as always answering the most common class, or nearest-centroid on simple features. With three people, guessing gives about 33%; a result is only meaningful relative to that. A confusion matrix shows, for each true person (rows), what the model answered (columns). The diagonal is correct; the off-diagonal cells show which people get mixed up.

Overfitting means the model has memorised the training data, including its noise, and does worse on new data. The sign is a large gap between training accuracy and validation accuracy. Remedies: more data from more sessions, simpler models, fewer features, regularisation. Public data (the FootprintID dataset: 10 people, 5 geophones, 8 walking speeds repo) is used first to build and test the pipeline before our own floor data exists; results on it say little about our floor.

Running a model on the ESP32-S3

Quantisation. A trained model stores weights as 32-bit floats. Converting them to 8-bit integers (int8), with scales and zero points stored per tensor or per channel, makes the model about four times smaller and often cheaper to run on a microcontroller (speed is not guaranteed), at the price of a small loss of precision. The loss must be measured: re-evaluate the quantised model on the test set. The conversion needs a representative dataset to choose the scales.

TensorFlow Lite Micro is a small runtime that runs such models on a microcontroller without an operating system. The exported file goes to packs/<id>/model.tflite repo. The runtime needs a working memory block (the "tensor arena"), which can live in internal RAM or PSRAM.

Appliance pack: anomaly detection

A machine has no labelled "faults" to learn from; you only know what normal looks like. The approach: record a baseline spectrum for each machine state (for example idle and running), as the average strength in several frequency bands (spectrum_bands_v1) with its normal spread. Every 10 s the node computes the same bands and measures how many standard deviations (σ) they differ from baseline. Beyond the alert level (starting value 4σ, with a baseline of 5 cycles repo) the "abnormal vibration" output turns on. The exact definition of a cycle and how bands are combined is not fixed yet. The target is to flag an induced imbalance in 9 of 10 runs.

Fall pack

An impact trigger (a level threshold) cuts a window of 500 ms before and 3000 ms after. A classifier labels it fall, object_drop, jump or other. Then a rule engine checks stillness: if the label is a fall and no footsteps (events of the footstep pack) follow for 20 s, "Fall detected" is raised repo. The target is at least 95% of dummy falls caught with fewer than 1 false alarm per week. False alarms are the hard part: many everyday impacts look like falls.

Say it to a judge

We train simple classifiers on features from walking recordings and always split by session, so a test result is on days the model has never seen. We compare with a baseline, and the system can answer "unknown" for people it was not enrolled with. Our Year 1 targets are stated up front; we have not claimed a result yet.

Self-check

1. Why is a random split of windows wrong for person identification?

Neighbouring windows from one walk are almost identical, so test data leaks into training and the score is inflated. Split by session or day.

2. What accuracy does random guessing give among three people?

About 33%.

3. What is a confusion matrix good for?

It shows which classes are mixed up with which, not just the overall score.

4. What does int8 quantisation trade?

A model about four times smaller and faster for a small, measurable loss of precision.

5. Describe the fall decision chain.

Integration: Wi-Fi, MQTT, Home Assistant

The node tells the home what it detected, not what it measured. Events travel over the local network and appear in Home Assistant without any cloud service.

Wi-Fi

The ESP32-S3 has 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi (802.11 b/g/n) built in datasheet. The node joins the home network as a station, like a phone.

MQTT

MQTT is a lightweight publish/subscribe protocol on top of TCP. Devices do not talk to each other directly. A broker (here Mosquitto) receives messages, each published to a topic, a text path such as a/b/c. Any client that has subscribed to that topic receives the message. The node publishes; Home Assistant subscribes. Features worth knowing: three quality-of-service levels (0 at most once, 1 at least once, 2 exactly once), retained messages (the broker keeps the last one and gives it to new subscribers), and a last will message (the broker announces a client that disappeared). The default port is 1883, or 8883 with TLS. Usernames and passwords for the broker are a setup task that is not decided in the repo.

Home Assistant MQTT discovery

Home Assistant can create entities automatically. The node publishes, once and retained, a small JSON discovery message to a topic under homeassistant/ that names the entity, its type, its state topic and its device class. Home Assistant reads it and the entity appears. The pack manifest's outputs map directly to this: entity (sensor, binary_sensor, event), name and device_class (for example safety for "Fall detected" and problem for "Abnormal vibration") repo. Only events and states travel over MQTT; raw samples stay on the card.

Say it to a judge

The node publishes only events, such as the last person or a fall, over MQTT to a local broker, and Home Assistant discovers the entities by itself from our pack manifests. Nothing leaves the home network and raw vibration data stays on the card.

Self-check

1. What does the broker do?

It receives published messages and forwards each one to every client subscribed to its topic.

2. What is a retained message and why use it for discovery?

The broker stores the last retained message on a topic and gives it to new subscribers, so Home Assistant finds the entity even if it starts after the node.

3. How do pack outputs map to Home Assistant?

Each output's entity type, name and device class become the discovery message's fields.

Hands-on skills

The first rig stands or falls on a few careful hours at the bench. Read this before the first soldering session, and practise on a spare chip or a cheap TSSOP board if you can.

Soldering the 0.65 mm TSSOP-20

The ADS131M04 in the PW package has 20 pins at 0.65 mm pitch (the pitch of the pins is the distance from one pin centre to the next), so the gap between pins is well under a millimetre. The adapter board spreads them to a DIP-width pattern. The typical method is drag soldering with plenty of flux typical practice:

  1. Prepare. Clean the adapter pads. Put a thin layer of tin on the pads, or none, as you prefer, and apply flux generously. Flux lets solder flow onto the metal and keeps neighbouring pins from bridging.
  2. Align and tack. Check pin 1 (the dot on the chip) against the adapter's marking. Hold the chip in place with tweezers and tack one corner pin, check alignment, then tack the diagonally opposite pin.
  3. Drag. Add flux again. Load a small amount of solder on a fine tip and draw it along the pins in one slow motion. Surface tension pulls solder onto each pin and pad and away from the gaps.
  4. Fix bridges. Add flux and use solder wick (braid) to remove excess solder from any bridge.
  5. Clean the flux residue with isopropyl alcohol and a brush.

Do not overheat: long contact times can damage the chip and lift pads. The project ordered three chips because the first attempt may fail.

Inspect before you power up

  • Loupe or microscope (at least 10x): look along the rows for bridges, for pins that sit on the pad but are not wetted (dull, ball-shaped solder), and for bent pins.
  • Multimeter in continuity or resistance mode. Check each pair of neighbouring pins: they should not be connected, except pins you know are tied on the board. Check from each pin to its pad on the adapter header: this proves the joint. Then check AVDD to AGND and DVDD to DGND (see below).

The 3.2 × 2.5 mm oscillator

The QTM325 is a ceramic-package SMD part, 3.2 mm by 2.5 mm and 1.0 mm high datasheet. For the breadboard it is mounted on a small oscillator adapter board (the common 7050, 5032 and 3225 footprint adapters; the 3225 size matches this part). Its pin 1 is the enable input: the output is on when pin 1 is at logic 1 or left open, off at logic 0 (below 30% of VDD; above 70% of VDD counts as high) datasheet. The usual layout for this package is pin 2 GND, pin 3 output, pin 4 VDD typical; check the dimensions drawing on page 2 of the datasheet. Tie pin 1 to VDD.

  • Use tweezers and flux. Tin one adapter pad lightly, place the part, reflow that corner, then solder the rest. Keep the iron time short.
  • The datasheet's reflow profile peaks at 260 °C for about 10 s, which is for an oven; with an iron, short contact is the point.
  • Decouple the oscillator's VDD with its own small ceramic capacitor right at the pins.
  • Check pin 1 and VDD before powering: a mixed-up VDD and output pin kills the part.

Multimeter checks before power-up

  1. 3.3 V rail to GND: no short (it should read well above a few ohms and may charge up slowly because of the capacitors).
  2. Check the supply wiring against section 8: 10 Ω resistor in series with AVDD (read about 10 Ω across it with the board unpowered), AVDD to AGND and DVDD to DGND not shorted.
  3. AINxN pins to AGND where intended; the geophone coil reads about 375 Ω (375 Ω ±2.5%) datasheet, and about 273 Ω with the 1 kΩ shunt in parallel calculated.
  4. Power up with only the 3.3 V and measure: DVDD about 3.3 V, AVDD about 35 mV lower (section 8), the oscillator VDD 3.3 V. Touch nothing with the board powered except the probes.

Scope checks: seeing the clock

Before any firmware, put a scope probe on CLKIN (pin 17). Use the probe's ground spring or a very short ground lead; a long ground lead rings and shows false overshoot. Expected values datasheet calculated:

ESD care

Static discharge can kill chips silently. The ADS131M04 is rated 2000 V human-body-model and 500 V charged-device-model datasheet, which is good for a chip in a factory but not for a bare chip in a dry room. Touch a grounded metal object before handling parts, keep chips in their bags until use, avoid handling pins, use a wrist strap and an anti-static mat if available, and discharge long cables (geophone leads) before plugging them in.

Judge questions

Answer each question aloud before you open the answer. The answers are short on purpose. They never claim a result the project has not measured: say "target" for targets and "datasheet value" for datasheet values.

1. What does Rezonating do, in one minute?

It is one vibration-sensing node with swappable, self-identifying sensor heads. Software packs decide what it detects. Year 1 has three packs: who is walking (from floor vibration), whether someone fell, and whether a machine vibrates abnormally. Targets for the end of Year 1: at least 85% correct with three people on our own floor; at least 95% of dummy falls caught with fewer than one false alarm per week; an induced imbalance flagged in 9 of 10 runs.

2. Why no camera and no microphone?

The product is built on the idea that a home can be understood without images or sound. A vibration sensor records neither faces nor speech. We are honest that footstep patterns can still identify a person, which is why the recordings use consent and pseudonyms.

3. Why a geophone for floors and an accelerometer for machines?

A geophone outputs a voltage proportional to velocity, and for small, low-frequency floor motion velocity gives much more signal than acceleration does (acceleration = 2π f × velocity). For 1 µm/s at 20 Hz the acceleration is only 12.8 µg, below the ADXL355's noise of 250 µg in a 100 Hz band, while the geophone chain sees 20.9 µV, about 27 times its noise. A machine is the opposite case: the accelerometer mounts on the housing and sees large, higher-frequency vibration.

4. Why simultaneous sampling?

The ADS131M04 samples all four channels at the same instant. Differences between sensors are then real differences in the floor, not conversion delays, which matters for comparing sensors and for the later localisation of events. The Year 1 packs do not yet depend on it.

5. Why a 24-bit converter?

Not for 24 usable bits: at gain 32 and 1 kSPS the datasheet gives 91 dB dynamic range, 16.6 effective bits. The reason is range: tiny footsteps and a harder stomp can both be recorded without switching gain, and the quantisation steps (4.5 nV at gain 32) are far below the noise (0.77 µV).

6. Why an external clock?

The ADS131M04 has no internal oscillator; it requires a continuous clock on CLKIN. An 8.192 MHz oscillator gives round data rates (1000 SPS exactly at OSR 4096), is independent of the processor, and its accuracy (±50 ppm or ±25 ppm by option) sets the sample-rate accuracy.

7. Why the ESP32-S3 N16R8?

Two cores at 240 MHz, DMA and interrupts to read the ADC without losing samples, Wi-Fi for MQTT, and 8 MB of PSRAM for the sample buffer and later the model. It is cheap and has a mature toolchain (ESP-IDF).

8. How does the system treat a person it does not know?

The classifier has an "unknown" label, and a confidence threshold can reject uncertain cases. How well this works is to be measured. We will report it.

9. How is accuracy measured, and on which data?

On held-out test sessions the model never saw: we split by session or day, never by window, and always report a baseline next to the result. The target is on our own floor with three people. Public data (FootprintID) is used first to build the pipeline; results on it are not claimed for our floor. At the time of writing nothing has been measured.

10. What are the biggest risks and how will you know?

(1) Concrete slabs may damp footsteps too much: the first recordings show the signal-to-noise ratio directly. (2) Person identification may be weaker in a real home than in a lab: tested on a second floor type and on new days. (3) Fall false alarms: counted over weeks of running. (4) Hand-soldering the fine-pitch ADC: the ID-register read and the clock check tell us the same day.

11. What is new about it?

One node with swappable heads that identify themselves, and applications delivered as software packs rather than new hardware and firmware. Each ingredient is standard; the combination, and the discipline around it (one feature implementation in two languages, split by session, every sample counted), is the project's contribution.

12. What will Year 1 show, and what will it not?

It will show whether the three packs meet their targets on our own floor with our own hardware. It will not show performance in many homes, outdoors, on battery, or for later applications. Those are outside Year 1.

13. Why train your own models instead of a cloud service?

Full control of features and models, local operation without cloud, and the same features in Python and on the node. The models are small enough for the ESP32-S3 after int8 quantisation.

14. How do you know you did not lose samples?

Every sample set carries a sequence number and a timestamp from the data-ready interrupt; the ADC's status word and CRC are checked; a counter records any gap. The firmware reports dropped samples and never hides them.

15. How is privacy handled?

Everything runs locally; only events go to the local broker. Recordings require written consent, people are pseudonyms, the name list is never stored in the repository, and raw recordings are never committed.

Gain 32: full scale ±37.5 mV; 1 LSB = 4.47 nV; noise 0.77 µV rms at 1 kSPS (91 dB dynamic range).

2. What is the right answer if a judge asks "what accuracy do you reach?"

The target is at least 85% with three people on our own floor; no model has been evaluated yet, and we say so.
